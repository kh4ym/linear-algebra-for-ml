# Part 0 — Start here (3 primers)

Part 0 gives a complete beginner the three things every later lesson silently assumes: the **frame** (a row of data is a point, a model is a map that moves points), the **symbols** (brackets, subscripts, Σ, ℝⁿ, m×n, ᵀ, ⁻¹, ‖v‖, Greek letters, f(x)), and the **unit circle** (what cos θ and sin θ are, so rotation matrices and cosine similarity are read off a picture, never memorized). Every symbol is born from a picture before it is used anywhere else in the course. Recommended order: p0-01 → p0-02 → p0-03 (about 25 minutes). Nothing here requires algebra beyond arithmetic.

Shared data for Part 0 (hard-coded, used again in p1-01 and p1-07):

| row | size (×10 m²) | rooms | price (×100 k) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.0 | 1 | 2.0 |
| 2 | 4.5 | 2 | 3.1 |
| 3 | 6.0 | 3 | 4.2 |
| 4 | 2.0 | 1 | 1.5 |
| 5 | 5.0 | 2 | 3.6 |
| 6 | 7.0 | 4 | 5.0 |
| 7 | 3.5 | 2 | 2.4 |
| 8 | 5.5 | 3 | 4.0 |

## p0-01-data-is-geometry — Why linear algebra? Rows are points, models are maps
**Part** 0 · **Tags** primer · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~7 · **Prereqs** —
**Goal.** The learner sees a table of numbers become a cloud of points, sees that dropping a column is a shadow, and sees a 20-second trailer of the maps (fit, rotate, split, bend) that the course will teach.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 3D (8 rows × 3 columns) · realSize: a tabular dataset has 10–1000 columns; an image row has 784; a text embedding 768–4096 · transfers: "row = point, column = axis, model = map" is exactly true in any number of columns; only the number of axes we can draw stops at 3.
**Can't show.**
- a table with more than 3 columns as a picture (we drop columns and say so)
- that real datasets have thousands of rows (we draw 8)
**Terms.** dataset — a table whose rows are the things we measured and whose columns are the measurements; feature — one column of a dataset, one measurement; point — a dot whose position is given by numbers along axes; axis — a labeled direction along which one number is read; model — a rule that moves or labels points; projection — flattening points onto fewer axes, like a shadow.
**State.** `rows` (8×3 table above) · `axes` ([colX, colY, colZ|null], which column feeds which axis) · `placed` (0..8, how many rows have flown into the picture) · `highlight` (row index or −1) · `drop` (0..1, progress of flattening onto the size–price plane) · `trailer` ({vignette 0..3, t}) · `marker` (yellow check marker position) · `view` ('2d'|'3d') · `cam` · `showTable` (bool)

**Steps.**
### 1. A table of eight apartments · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Here is a small dataset: eight apartments, three measurements each — size, number of rooms, price. Every machine-learning problem starts as a table like this one. This course is about one idea: <b>a row of numbers is a point</b>, and a model is something that moves points around.
Canvas: 2D view. Right half: the 8×3 table drawn with `g.label` in a grid (header row: size · rooms · price; the header "size" tinted green, "price" red). Left half: empty axes (`g.axes` with labels "size" on the green axis, "price" on the red axis, range 0–8). Rows fade in top to bottom over t.
Math panel: —

### 2. One row becomes one point · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Take row 1: size 3.0, price 2.0. Walk 3.0 along the green <b>size</b> axis, then 2.0 up the red <b>price</b> axis, and put a dot there. The row of the table and the dot are the same thing written two ways.
Canvas: row 1 highlights (yellow box). A yellow dot flies from the table cell to (3.0, 2.0) along a bezier path over t∈[0,0.6]; then `g.vector` dashed green from the origin to (3.0, 0) and dashed red from (3.0, 0) to (3.0, 2.0) with `g.dim` labels "3.0" and "2.0" (t∈[0.6,1]).
Math panel: "row 1: size 3.0 · price 2.0" with 3.0 in green, 2.0 in red.

### 3. Eight rows, eight points · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Do it for every row. The table turns into a <b>cloud of points</b>. Look at the cloud and you already see something the table hides: bigger apartments cost more, and the points nearly line up. A picture tells you what a column of numbers cannot.
Canvas: rows 2–8 fly one by one (0.4 s each, `placed` counts up) to (size, price) as yellow `g.points`; the table rows dim as they leave. At t=1 a faint gold dashed line (least-squares fit through the 8 points, precomputed with `LA.num.lstsq`) fades in for one beat and out again.
Math panel: "8 rows → 8 points"

### 4. A third column, a third axis · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: There is a third column, rooms. Give it its own axis, the blue one pointing out of the screen. Each point lifts to its rooms value. Three columns, three axes, one point per row. Drag to orbit and look around the cloud.
Canvas: view tweens to 3D (`s.view` → '3d', `s.cam` eases from top-down to yaw 35°, pitch 25°, dist 14). `g.axes` with the blue axis labeled "rooms"; each point rises along a blue dashed segment from z=0 to z=rooms over t∈[0.3,0.9]. Grid floor via `g.grid` ghost. `interactive` orbit is allowed only in the playground; here the camera eases automatically.
Math panel: "row 3: size 6.0 · rooms 3 · price 4.2" with the three numbers in green, blue, red.

### 5. Table and picture are one object · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: A common belief: "the table is the data; the picture is just an illustration." Test it. Change one number in the table and the point moves. Delete a row and a point disappears. Nothing is lost either way. The table <i>is</i> the cloud.
Canvas: left label "table ≠ picture" with a red ✕ (`g.marker.cross`) that appears at t=0.5. Row 5's price cell counts from 3.6 to 4.6 (t∈[0,0.4]) while point 5 slides up along the red direction in sync; then row 4 fades out (t∈[0.5,0.7]) and point 4 fades out with it; both restore at t∈[0.8,1].
Math panel: "row 5: price 3.6 → 4.6" live.

### 6. Dropping a column is a shadow · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Suppose a model ignores rooms. Then it sees the cloud flattened onto the size–price wall — every point drops straight down, like a shadow. Flattening is cheap and useful, and it loses something: two apartments with the same size and price but different rooms become one dot. We will call this a <b>projection</b> and always say what it lost.
Canvas: camera eases to look along the blue axis (yaw 0°, pitch 0° → wall view). Each point slides along a blue dashed residual (`g.line` dashed gray) to its (size, 0, price) shadow drawn magenta (`drop` 0→1). Label at t=1: "shadow of 3 columns on 2 · lost: rooms".
Math panel: "(size, rooms, price) → (size, price)" with rooms struck through in blue.

### 7. Four things a model can do · 20 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: What will you be able to do by the end? Four previews, five seconds each. <b>Fit</b>: a line that predicts price from size. <b>Rotate</b>: find the direction the cloud is longest in. <b>Split</b>: a plane that separates cheap from expensive. <b>Bend</b>: a sheet that curves until a flat cut separates two rings. Each one is a map on points.
Canvas: four vignettes, `trailer.vignette` 0..3, 5 s each. (0) 2D size–price points with the gold fit line sweeping in from horizontal to the least-squares slope; residual segments dashed gray. (1) the 3D cloud with a gold long axis (PC1 from `LA.num.pca`) growing from the mean while the camera orbits 60°. (2) points recolored sky blue (price < 3.3) and rose (≥ 3.3); a teal plane (`g.planes`) sweeps and settles where it separates them. (3) 2D: two concentric rings of points (`LA.rng(12)` circles, inner r 0.5, outer r 1.5, 40+40) → view lifts to 3D, the plane bends into a bowl (`g.surface` z = x²+y²), a gold plane cuts it at z = 1. Each vignette labeled bottom-left: "Part 3 · fit", "Part 4 · PCA", "Part 1 · classifier", "Part 2 · neural layer".
Math panel: the four words fit · rotate · split · bend, the current one highlighted.

### 8. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: Three things to keep. A row of a table is a point; the columns are axes. Dropping a column is a shadow, and a shadow loses something. A model is a map that moves, rotates, splits or bends the cloud of points. Everything else in this course is how to write such maps down.
Canvas: three mini-cards (`g.polygon` panels with `g.label`): card 1 a tiny table with an arrow to three dots; card 2 three dots dropping to shadows; card 3 a small grid being sheared with a gold line through it.
Math panel: —

**Playground.** controls: preset · `axes` · {size–price, size–rooms, rooms–price, all three (3D)} · "Axes"; toggle · `showTable` · on/off · "Show table"; toggle · `view` · 2D/3D · "3D"; slider · `rows[highlight][2]` · 0–8 step 0.1 · "Price of highlighted row"; preset · `highlight` · rows 1–8 · "Highlight row"; button · Reset. Presets: "Size vs price", "All three columns", "Shadow (drop rooms)". Live: points, dashed component lines of the highlighted row, the table cell values, the gold fit line (2D only).
**Checks.**
1. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Drag the yellow marker to where row 5 belongs in the size–price picture." · setup: 2D view, axes size–price, points 1–4 and 6–8 placed, row 5 highlighted in the table, marker at (1, 6) · judge: `dist(marker, [5.0, 3.6]) < LA.TOL.pos` · hint: "Read size along the green axis first, then price up the red one." · solution: marker tweens to (5.0, 3.6).
2. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Orbit until you look straight down the blue rooms axis, so the picture becomes the size–price plot." · setup: 3D view, all points placed, camera at yaw 40°, pitch 30° · judge: `angle(cam.forward, ±k̂) < 2·LA.TOL.angleDeg` · hint: "Drag the view until the blue axis shrinks to a dot." · solution: camera tweens to pitch 90° (top-down, k̂ toward the viewer).
3. kind: entry · prompt: "Type the three numbers of row 3 as a point (size, rooms, price)." · bind: `probe` (3-vector) · answer: [6.0, 3, 4.2] · tol: LA.TOL.entry · live: an orange point drawn at the typed coordinates with dashed component lines; glows gold when it coincides with point 3.
**ML tie-in.** Step 7 previews p3-05 (least squares), p4-10 (PCA), p1-09 (a neuron's boundary) and p2-12 (a neural layer). Bridge: every ML algorithm in the course takes a cloud of points like this one and either fits a line through it, finds its long axis, cuts it with a plane, or bends the space it lives in until a cut works. Part 0 only establishes that the cloud and the table are one object, so that from now on "do something to the data" means "move the points".
**Recap cards.** (1) "Row = point, column = axis" — table → three dots. (2) "Dropping a column is a shadow; shadows lose something" — dots falling onto a wall with dashed residuals. (3) "A model is a map on points" — a grid being sheared with a gold line.
**Engine needs.** A table primitive is not needed: draw with `g.label` in a screen-anchored block (`anchor` option). Camera forward vector must be derivable from `s.cam` (yaw, pitch) for check 2.

## p0-02-reading-the-symbols — How to read the symbols
**Part** 0 · **Tags** primer · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p0-01-data-is-geometry
**Goal.** The learner can read every symbol the course will use — brackets, subscripts, two-index entries, m×n, Σ, ℝⁿ, f(x), ᵀ, ⁻¹, ‖v‖, Greek letters — as the name of a picture they have seen.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: a digit classifier is written ŷ = Wx + b with W 10×784, x 784×1, b 10×1 · transfers: the symbols mean exactly the same thing at any size; only the number of cells grows.
**Can't show.**
- a 784-cell strip at readable size (we draw 784 thin cells and print the first 12 values)
**Terms.** vector — a list of numbers written in a tall bracket, also an arrow or a point; entry — one number inside a bracket; subscript — the small number that says which entry; matrix — a rectangular grid of numbers in a bracket; shape — rows × columns of a matrix, rows first; sum (Σ) — add up every entry in a strip; real numbers (ℝ) — all the ordinary numbers on a number line; ℝⁿ — all lists of n real numbers, the space our points live in; function — a rule that turns an input into exactly one output; transpose — flip a matrix across its diagonal so rows become columns; inverse — the rule that undoes another rule; norm — the length of a vector.
**State.** `v` (2-vector, default [3, 2]) · `A` (2×3 matrix, default [[1, 4, 7],[2, 5, 8]]) · `cell` ([i, j] highlighted entry or null) · `strip` (array of n values, default [2, −1, 3, 4]) · `summed` (0..n cells added) · `n` (strip length for the ℝⁿ step: 2, 3, 784) · `flip` (0..1 transpose progress) · `movie` (−1..1: forward progress of the "stretch by 2" movie; negative = reversed) · `greek` (index of the Greek letter shown) · `ring` (draggable highlight ring position for check 2) · `tiles` (shape-tile order for check 3)

**Steps.**
### 1. A bracket holds a point · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Here is the point from the last lesson, 3 along the green axis and 2 along the red. Write the two numbers in a tall bracket, green on top, red below. That bracket is a <b>vector</b>: a list of numbers that is also a point and also an arrow. We name it <span class="c-v">v</span>.
Canvas: 2D axes range −1..5. Yellow point at (3, 2) with dashed green/red component lines (`g.vector` with `components:'xy'`). Over t∈[0.3,0.8] the labels "3" and "2" fly (`ctx.fx.fly`) toward the math panel. At t=1 a yellow arrow from the origin to the point fades in with label "v".
Math panel: `v = [3; 2]` as a tall bracket (`tex.vec`), top entry green, bottom red.

### 2. Subscripts say which entry · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The small number under a letter is a <b>subscript</b>. It picks an entry: <span class="c-v">v</span><sub>1</sub> is the first entry, 3, the green one; <span class="c-v">v</span><sub>2</sub> is the second, 2, the red one. Subscripts are addresses, not powers and not multiplication.
Canvas: the bracket is also drawn on canvas (`g.label` block). A pulsing ring (`g.circle`) sits on the top cell while the green dashed component highlights (t<0.5), then moves to the bottom cell while the red component highlights (t>0.5).
Math panel: `v₁ = 3` (green), `v₂ = 2` (red), each appearing as its cell is ringed.

### 3. Two subscripts: row, then column · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A grid of numbers in a bracket is a <b>matrix</b>, written with a capital letter, <i>A</i>. Each entry needs two addresses: <i>a</i><sub>23</sub> means row 2, column 3 — the row number always comes first. Read it like a street grid: go down to the row, then across to the column.
Canvas: a 2×3 grid of cells with values 1 4 7 / 2 5 8 (`g.polygon` cells + `g.label`). A horizontal bar sweeps down to row 2 (t∈[0.1,0.4]), a vertical bar sweeps right to column 3 (t∈[0.4,0.7]), the cell "8" pulses gold (t>0.7). Columns are tinted green, red, blue left to right (matrix columns are colored by the basis vector they record; the learner meets why in Part 2).
Math panel: `A = [[1,4,7],[2,5,8]]` with `a₂₃ = 8` highlighted.

### 4. Shape: rows × columns · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: The <b>shape</b> of a matrix is rows × columns, rows first: this <i>A</i> is 2×3, two rows and three columns. Is a 3×2 matrix the same thing? No. Flip the roles and the bracket is tall instead of wide; the entries live in different cells. Shape is read in a fixed order.
Canvas: left, the 2×3 bracket with a `g.dim` bracket "2 rows" on its side and "3 columns" below. Right, a 3×2 bracket appears (t∈[0.3,0.6]) with its own dims. A label "2×3 = 3×2?" between them gets a red ✕ at t=0.8 as a gray outline of the 2×3 bracket tries to overlay the 3×2 one and does not fit.
Math panel: "A is 2×3 · B is 3×2" with the shape numbers colored (rows plain, columns underlined).

### 5. Σ means "add the strip" · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A row of four numbers: 2, −1, 3, 4. The capital Greek letter <b>Σ</b> (sigma) means "add them all up". Written out, Σ<sub><i>i</i></sub> <i>s</i><sub><i>i</i></sub> says: let <i>i</i> run over every address, and add the entry at each one. Watch the cells slide onto a pile and the total grow.
Canvas: a 1×4 strip (`g.heat2d` with 1×4 grid, diverging palette, values printed). One cell per 0.2 s slides to a "pile" box on the right; a running total label counts 2 → 1 → 4 → 8. The pile box ends labeled "Σ = 8".
Math panel: `Σᵢ sᵢ = 2 + (−1) + 3 + 4 = 8`, each term appearing as its cell lands.

### 6. ℝ, ℝ², ℝⁿ · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: <b>ℝ</b> is the set of all ordinary numbers, the number line. <b>ℝ²</b> is all pairs of numbers — the whole plane, where <span class="c-v">v</span> lives. ℝ³ is all triples, our 3D room. <b>ℝ⁷⁸⁴</b> is all lists of 784 numbers: one synthetic digit image. The picture stops at 3. The formula does not.
Canvas: t∈[0,0.3]: a number line with a dot at 2.5, label "ℝ". t∈[0.3,0.6]: the plane with the yellow point, label "ℝ²". t∈[0.6,0.8]: a 1×3 strip [2, 1, 1.5], label "ℝ³ — three numbers, a point in a room". t∈[0.8,1]: a 1×784 thin strip (`g.heat2d`, gray palette) from a synthetic "7" (`LA.rng.syntheticDigits(seed 2)`, 28×28 flattened row by row) with the first 12 values printed and a label "ℝ⁷⁸⁴ · one point we cannot draw".
Math panel: "ℝ · ℝ² · ℝ³ · ℝⁿ: a list of n numbers", the current one highlighted.

### 7. f(x): a rule with an input and an output · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: <b>f(x)</b> reads "f of x": the rule <i>f</i> applied to the input <i>x</i>. It is not multiplication. Here <i>f</i> doubles the distance from the origin. Feed it the point <span class="c-v">v</span> and out comes <i>f</i>(<span class="c-v">v</span>), a new point. Later, rules that move points are called transformations.
Canvas: a rounded box labeled "f" in the middle; the yellow point enters from the left along a path (t∈[0,0.4]), the box pulses, and a yellow point exits to the right at twice the distance (t∈[0.5,0.9]), drawn on a small plane with both the input (ghost) and output (solid), connected by a dashed arrow.
Math panel: `f(v) = 2v`, then `f([3; 2]) = [6; 4]` with colored entries.

### 8. ᵀ flips rows and columns · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The small <b>ᵀ</b> means <b>transpose</b>: flip the matrix across its diagonal, so each row becomes a column. The 2×3 matrix <i>A</i> becomes the 3×2 matrix <i>A</i><sup>ᵀ</sup>, and the entry at row 2, column 3 moves to row 3, column 2. A standing vector transposed is a lying-down vector.
Canvas: the 2×3 grid; a dashed diagonal from the top-left corner; every cell slides along the straight path to its mirrored position (`flip` 0→1) keeping its color tint; the bracket reshapes from wide to tall. Then (t>0.8) the yellow [3; 2] bracket rotates to lie down as [3 2].
Math panel: `Aᵀ = [[1,2],[4,5],[7,8]]` and `vᵀ = [3 2]`.

### 9. ⁻¹ plays the movie backwards · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The small <b>⁻¹</b> means <b>inverse</b>: the rule that undoes another rule. On numbers, 2<sup>−1</sup> = ½ undoes multiplying by 2. On pictures, if <i>f</i> stretches the grid, <i>f</i><sup>−1</sup> is the same movie played backwards, and <i>f</i><sup>−1</sup>(<i>f</i>(<span class="c-v">v</span>)) brings <span class="c-v">v</span> home.
Canvas: a grid (`g.grid`) stretches by 2 along the green axis over the ghost grid (`movie` 0→1 during t∈[0,0.45]); the yellow point rides along. A label "f" shows. Then the movie reverses (t∈[0.55,1]) with label "f⁻¹" and the point returns exactly to the ghost position; a small green ✓ appears.
Math panel: `2⁻¹ · 2 = 1` then `f⁻¹(f(v)) = v`.

### 10. ‖v‖ is the length · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Double bars around a vector, <b>‖<span class="c-v">v</span>‖</b>, mean its <b>length</b> — how long the arrow is, measured with a tape from the origin to the tip. For [3; 2] it is about 3.61. How to compute it from the entries is the subject of a later lesson; for now, learn to read it.
Canvas: the yellow arrow; a `g.dim` measuring line runs along it from origin to tip (t∈[0,0.6]) and the label "‖v‖ ≈ 3.61" appears at t=0.7.
Math panel: `‖v‖ ≈ 3.61`

### 11. Greek letters are just names · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Greek letters are names for numbers, chosen by habit. <b>θ</b> (theta) is an angle. <b>λ</b> (lambda) is a stretch factor. <b>σ</b> (sigma, lowercase) is a size or spread. <b>η</b> (eta) is a step size. You already met capital Σ, which is an instruction, not a name. Each shows next to its picture.
Canvas: four small panels, each lighting for 1 s: θ with a `g.arc` between two arrows; λ with an arrow stretching from length 1 to 2.5 and label "λ = 2.5"; σ with a cloud of points and a dashed circle of radius σ; η with an arrow taking a short step along a dashed direction, label "η".
Math panel: θ · λ · σ · η, each highlighted in turn with its one-word meaning.

### 12. One line of ML, symbol by symbol · 5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Here is the formula of a digit classifier: ŷ = <i>W</i><span class="c-v">x</span> + <span class="c-b">b</span>. You can now read every mark. <span class="c-v">x</span> is a vector in ℝ<sup>784</sup>; <i>W</i> is a 10×784 matrix; <span class="c-b">b</span> is a vector of 10; ŷ is the output vector. The shapes chain: (10×784)(784×1) gives 10×1. Part 2 teaches what the multiplication does.
Canvas: the formula drawn large with `g.label`. Shape tiles under each symbol: "10×784" (wide bracket outline), "784×1" (tall thin strip, the synthetic 7), "10×1". The inner 784s slide together and snap (t∈[0.5,0.8]); a 10-cell output strip (`g.bars`) lights with one tall bar at index 7 (t>0.85).
Math panel: `ŷ = Wx + b` with shapes beneath each symbol.

### 13. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: You can now read the course's alphabet. Brackets hold vectors and matrices; subscripts are addresses, row before column; shape is rows × columns. Σ adds a strip, ℝⁿ is "lists of n numbers", f(x) is a rule applied, ᵀ flips, ⁻¹ reverses, ‖v‖ measures. When a symbol appears later, its picture will appear first.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) a bracket with a ringed cell and "a₂₃"; (2) a strip sliding onto a pile with "Σ"; (3) a grid stretching and un-stretching with "f, f⁻¹".
Math panel: —

**Playground.** controls: matrix · `A` · 2×3 entries −9..9 · "Matrix A"; preset · `cell` · {a₁₁ … a₂₃} · "Highlight entry"; slider · `n` (strip length) · 2–12 · "Strip length"; toggle · `flip` · 0/1 · "Transpose"; slider · `movie` · −1..1 · "Play f / f⁻¹"; button · Reset. Presets: "Identity-ish", "Random strip", "Tall 3×2". Live: the ringed cell and its `aᵢⱼ` readout, the Σ total, the transposed grid, the stretched grid.
**Checks.**
1. kind: entry · prompt: "Type the entry a₂₃ of the matrix on screen." · bind: `guess` (scalar) · setup: `A = [[3, −2, 6],[5, 1, 7]]` drawn as a grid; a gold ring on the cell whose value equals the typed number, if any · answer: 7 · tol: LA.TOL.entry · hint: "Row first: the second row. Then across to the third column."
2. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Drag the ring onto the entry a₁₃." · setup: same A, ring starts on a₂₁ · judge: `dist(ring, center(cell 1,3)) < LA.TOL.pos` (cells are 1 world unit) · hint: "Row 1 is the top row; column 3 is the rightmost." · solution: ring tweens to cell (1,3) and the cell pulses gold.
3. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Arrange the three shape tiles so the product (2×3)(3×1) is written in a valid order." · setup: tiles "2×3", "3×1", "= 2×1" shuffled on a shelf; drag tiles into three slots · judge: slot order equals [2×3, 3×1, = 2×1]; inner numbers touching glow green, otherwise a red ✕ between them · hint: "The inner numbers must touch: columns of the left tile = rows of the right." · solution: tiles slide into order.
**ML tie-in.** Step 12 previews p2-02 (matrix × vector) and p6-01 (batches and shapes). Bridge: ML papers and code are written in exactly this alphabet — `W @ x + b`, shapes in error messages, `x.T`, `Σᵢ` in loss functions. A learner who can read the marks as pictures (a strip, a grid, a flip, a reverse movie) never has to look a symbol up again; the glossary drawer holds every term from this lesson.
**Recap cards.** (1) "Addresses: vᵢ, aᵢⱼ — row first" — a grid with a ringed cell. (2) "Σ adds the strip; ℝⁿ is lists of n numbers" — strip sliding onto a pile. (3) "ᵀ flips, ⁻¹ reverses, ‖v‖ measures" — a grid stretching then un-stretching with a tape measure.

## p0-03-angles-and-circles — Angles, the unit circle, cos and sin
**Part** 0 · **Tags** primer · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~8 · **Prereqs** p0-02-reading-the-symbols
**Goal.** The learner can read cos θ and sin θ as the x- and y-coordinates of a point on the unit circle, knows the landmark angles, and sees that a rotated î is exactly that point.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: cosine similarity compares 768-dimensional embeddings; a 2D rotation block appears inside every rotary position encoding · transfers: the angle between two arrows is defined the same way in any dimension (via the dot product, Part 1); the unit-circle picture is all the trigonometry this course needs.
**Can't show.**
- an angle between two vectors in ℝ⁷⁶⁸ (it exists; it is computed, not seen)
**Terms.** angle — the amount of turn between two arrows from the same point, measured in degrees; degree — 1/360 of a full turn; radian — the angle whose arc on the unit circle has length 1; about 57°; unit circle — the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin; cosine (cos θ) — the x-coordinate of the unit-circle point at angle θ; sine (sin θ) — the y-coordinate of that point; rotation — turning every arrow by the same angle about the origin.
**State.** `theta` (degrees, default 40) · `showArc` · `showDrops` (component drop lines) · `showGraph` (cos/sin graph strip below the circle) · `landmark` (index into [0, 30, 45, 60, 90, 180, 270]) · `rotI` (0..1: how far î has rotated toward θ) · `rotJ` (same for ĵ) · `pair` ({a, b} angles of two unit arrows for the alignment step) · `wrongBelief` (0..1 progress of the misconception plot)

**Steps.**
### 1. An angle is an amount of turn · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two arrows from the origin. The <b>angle</b> between them is how far you turn one to reach the other, measured in <b>degrees</b>: a quarter turn is 90°, a square corner; a half turn is 180°; a full turn brings you back, 360°. We draw the angle as a small arc and call it θ.
Canvas: 2D, range −1.5..1.5. The green î fixed; a yellow unit arrow sweeps from 0° to 40° (t∈[0,0.5]) with `g.arc` between them labeled "θ = 40°". Then a ghost arrow continues to 90° (a small square marker at the corner), 180°, 360° with labels (t∈[0.5,1]).
Math panel: `θ = 40°`

### 2. Radians: the arc's own length · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Code measures angles in <b>radians</b>: the length of the arc on a circle of radius 1. A full turn is the circumference, 2π ≈ 6.28; a half turn is π; a quarter turn is π/2 ≈ 1.57. We will say degrees and show radians beside them, because every formula uses radians.
Canvas: the unit circle (`g.circle`) appears; the arc from 0° to θ thickens and a `g.dim` label shows its length "0.70". Then the arc grows to 90° ("π/2 ≈ 1.57"), 180° ("π ≈ 3.14"), 360° ("2π ≈ 6.28").
Math panel: `θ = 40° = 0.70 rad` with `360° = 2π`.

### 3. A point walks the unit circle · 4 s · flags: interactive
Narration: The <b>unit circle</b> is every arrow of length exactly 1. Put a yellow point on it at angle θ from the green î. As θ grows the point walks counter-clockwise around the circle. Drag it; the angle readout follows. Everything in trigonometry is about where this point is.
Canvas: the unit circle, the yellow point at (cos θ, sin θ) with a thin yellow radius arrow; `g.handle` on the point constrained to the circle; θ sweeps 0 → 40° automatically at t∈[0,0.6] if not dragged; arc with label.
Math panel: `θ` live in degrees and radians.

### 4. cos θ is the x-coordinate, sin θ is the y-coordinate · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Drop the point onto the axes. Its green shadow on the x-axis is <b>cos θ</b>; its red height is <b>sin θ</b>. That is the whole definition: cos is "how far along î", sin is "how far along ĵ", for a unit arrow at angle θ. At 40°, cos ≈ 0.77 and sin ≈ 0.64.
Canvas: dashed green drop from the point to the x-axis with `g.dim` "cos θ = 0.77" (t∈[0,0.5]); dashed red drop to the y-axis with "sin θ = 0.64" (t∈[0.5,1]). The point's bracket [cos θ; sin θ] appears beside it.
Math panel: `[cos θ; sin θ] = [0.77; 0.64]` (green, red), live with θ.

### 5. Landmarks you will see again · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Watch the point hop to the angles that appear everywhere. 0°: [1; 0]. 90°: [0; 1]. 180°: [−1; 0]. 270°: [0; −1]. 45°: both coordinates equal, about 0.71. 60°: [0.5; 0.87]; 30°: [0.87; 0.5]. The shadows swap roles as the point climbs.
Canvas: the point hops (0.6 s each, eased) through 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 45°, 60°, 30°; at each stop the green/red drops redraw and a small label with the pair stays as a dot on the circle. A table of the seven pairs fills on the right.
Math panel: the table θ → (cos θ, sin θ) with the current row highlighted.

### 6. cos does not grow with the angle · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: A tempting belief: "a bigger angle gives a bigger cosine." Watch the green shadow as the point passes 90°: it shrinks to zero, then turns negative. At 120°, cos θ = −0.5. Cosine measures how much the arrow still points along î, and past a quarter turn it points the other way.
Canvas: the point sweeps 0° → 180° (t∈[0,0.8]) while a graph strip under the circle (`g.lineChart`, x = θ, y = cos θ) draws live in green; a gray line "belief: cos grows" rises steadily and gets a red ✕ at θ = 120° where the true curve reads −0.5 (t=0.85). The red sin curve draws faintly for comparison.
Math panel: `cos 120° = −0.5 · sin 120° = 0.87`

### 7. Rotating î lands on that point · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Now rotate the green î itself by θ. Its tip lands exactly on the unit-circle point: the rotated î is [cos θ; sin θ]. Nothing to memorize — the two numbers are simply where the green arrow went. Part 2 writes this as the first column of a rotation matrix.
Canvas: the green î (length 1) rotates from 0° to θ (`rotI` 0→1); its ghost stays at [1; 0]; `g.arc`; at t=1 the tip coincides with the yellow point, which pulses, and the label "î → [cos θ; sin θ]" appears.
Math panel: `î ↦ [cos θ; sin θ] = [0.77; 0.64]`

### 8. ĵ is a quarter turn ahead · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The red ĵ starts a quarter turn ahead of î, at 90°. Rotate it by the same θ and it lands at 90° + θ: coordinates [−sin θ; cos θ]. Its shadow along the x-axis is the negative of î's height. Both rotated arrows stay perpendicular and stay length 1.
Canvas: the red ĵ rotates from 90° to 90° + θ in sync with a replay of î (`rotJ`); dashed drops show −sin θ (green, pointing left) and cos θ (red). A square `g.arc` right-angle marker sits between the rotated pair at t=1. Ghosts of both originals remain.
Math panel: `ĵ ↦ [−sin θ; cos θ] = [−0.64; 0.77]`

### 9. cos θ measures alignment · 4 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Take two unit arrows. The cosine of the angle between them says how aligned they are: 1 when they point the same way, 0 when perpendicular, −1 when opposite. Machine learning uses exactly this number to compare meanings: the arrows for "cat" and "kitten" have cosine near 0.9. Part 1 shows how to compute it without ever drawing the angle.
Canvas: two unit arrows, yellow "cat" and purple "kitten", at angles 20° and 45°; the arc between them with "cos 25° = 0.91"; the purple arrow sweeps to 110° ("cos 90° = 0") and 200° ("cos 180° = −1", label changes to "invoice"). A bar under the circle tracks the cosine value live.
Math panel: `cos(angle between) = 0.91 → 0 → −1`

### 10. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: The unit circle is all of trigonometry you need. A unit arrow at angle θ has coordinates [cos θ; sin θ]. Rotating î by θ lands there; rotating ĵ lands at [−sin θ; cos θ]. Cosine runs from 1 (same way) through 0 (perpendicular) to −1 (opposite) and is how ML compares directions.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) unit circle with the point, green and red drops; (2) î and ĵ rotated by θ with their brackets; (3) two arrows with an arc and a cosine bar.
