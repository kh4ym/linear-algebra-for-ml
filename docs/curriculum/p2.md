# Part 2 — Transformations and matrices (12 lessons)

Part 2 turns the learner's vectors into maps. Through-line: a linear transformation is a motion of the whole grid that keeps lines straight and the origin fixed, and it is completely recorded by where î and ĵ land — the columns of a matrix (p2-01). Multiplying a matrix by a vector is that recipe written out, in a column view and a row view (p2-02). The zoo of 2D maps is read off the grid (p2-03); doing one map after another multiplies matrices, in right-to-left order (p2-04); 3D adds a blue column and non-commuting rotations (p2-05). The determinant measures area/volume and orientation, with the cross product as its 3D companion (p2-06); the inverse is the movie played backwards and solves Ax = b (p2-07); rank, column space and null space say what survives and what is lost (p2-08); non-square matrices change dimension (p2-09); the transpose, outer products and symmetric matrices (p2-10); the affine map Wx + b and the homogeneous trick (p2-11); and the capstone, a neural layer as transformation plus fold, with an exactly-3D hidden space (p2-12). Recommended order is the id order. p2-01 is the engine author's reference lesson and is the most detailed storyboard here.

Conventions for this part: the transformed grid (`g.grid(M)`) is always drawn over the dashed ghost grid (`g.ghostGrid()`); the landing spots of î, ĵ, k̂ are the columns of the matrix and carry the same green/red/blue; a yellow `v` rides along when present; grid range 5 (2D) or a unit cube with a range-2 lattice (3D) unless stated.

Shared data: `circles120` (p2-12): `LA.rng(12)`, 60 inner points at radius 0.5 and 60 outer at radius 1.5, uniform angle, radial noise sd 0.1; `spirals200` (p2-12): `LA.rng(13)`, two arms, 1.5 turns, 100 points each, noise sd 0.08; `twoClouds` (p2-01 step 11): `LA.rng(21)`, 40 + 40 points, means ±[0.8, 0.8], sd 1.0 along [1, −1] and 0.25 along [1, 1].

## p2-01-linear-transformations — Linear transformations are matrices
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p1-03-linear-combinations-span, p0-03-angles-and-circles
**Goal.** The learner sees a linear transformation as a motion of the whole grid, knows it is fixed by where î and ĵ land, reads those landing spots as the columns of a matrix, and can predict where any vector goes from its recipe.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D (2×2) and 3D (3×3) · realSize: W is 784×128 in the first layer of a digit classifier · transfers: "columns are where the basis vectors land" and "the recipe is unchanged, only the ingredients moved" are exactly true for any shape; only the number of columns we can draw stops at 3.
**Can't show.**
- a 784×128 grid motion (we draw 2 and 3 columns and say the rule is identical)
**Terms.** transformation — a rule that takes every vector to a new vector; we watch it by moving the whole grid; linear transformation — a transformation that keeps grid lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced and keeps the origin fixed; equivalently L(u + v) = L(u) + L(v) and L(cv) = cL(v); matrix — the recording of a linear transformation: its columns are where î, ĵ (and k̂) land; column — one vertical strip of a matrix, the landing spot of one basis vector; landing spot — the vector a basis vector is carried to; identity — the transformation that moves nothing; its matrix has 1s on the diagonal.
**State.** `M` (2×2, default [[2, −1],[1, 1]]) · `M3` (3×3, default [[1.3, −0.5, 0],[0.75, 0.87, 0],[0, 0, 0.7]]) · `morph` (0..1: grid drawn under lerp(I, M, morph)) · `v` ([2, 1]) · `showGhost` (true) · `showLattice` (dots on integer points) · `showV` · `highlightCol` (null | 0 | 1) · `badCase` ('none' | 'warp' | 'translate') · `theta` (degrees, for the rotation step) · `cloud` (`twoClouds`, for the ML step) · `view` ('2d'|'3d') · `cam`

**Steps.**
### 1. Watch the whole grid move · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A <b>transformation</b> takes every vector to a new vector. One at a time that is hard to see, so we watch all of them at once: the whole grid moves. Here a transformation plays forward and back. The yellow <span class="c-v">v</span> and the green and red axis arrows ride with it.
Canvas: 2D range 5. `g.ghostGrid()` dashed gray; `g.grid(lerp(I, M, morph))` blue-gray with brighter axes; lattice dots (`g.points` at integer points, transformed); î, ĵ (`g.vector` with hats) and v drawn transformed. `morph` rises 0 → 1 (t∈[0, 0.45], ease in-out), holds, falls back to 0 (t∈[0.55, 1]).
Math panel: —

### 2. Linear: straight, parallel, evenly spaced, origin fixed · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: This motion is <b>linear</b>. Two things stay true while it plays: grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put. Stretching, squashing, turning and flipping are all allowed; bending and sliding are not. Every map in this course until Part 5 is linear.
Canvas: `morph` 0 → 1 slowly (t∈[0, 0.8]); two grid lines (x = 1 and x = 2) are highlighted to show they remain parallel and equally spaced; the origin pulses with a small label "origin stays"; at t > 0.85 the labels "straight · parallel · evenly spaced · origin fixed" appear one by one along the bottom.
Math panel: `L(u + v) = L(u) + L(v) · L(c·v) = c·L(v)` fading in at t > 0.9.

### 3. Two motions that are not linear · 5 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "any smooth motion of the grid is linear." Test two. A warp that bends lines: the rules fail, because a straight line of points no longer lands on a straight line. A slide that moves every point by the same amount: lines stay straight, but the origin moves, so L(0) ≠ 0. Both get a red ✕.
Canvas: left half: `badCase = 'warp'` — the grid is drawn through (x, y) ↦ (x + 0.3·y², y + 0.3·sin x) with `morph` 0 → 1 (t∈[0, 0.4]); label "lines bend" and `g.marker.cross` at t = 0.45. Right half: `badCase = 'translate'` — the grid slides by [1.5, 1] (t∈[0.5, 0.9]) over the ghost; the origin marker moves off the ghost origin with a `g.dim` to its old place; label "origin moved", red ✕ at t = 0.95. Both halves reset to the ghost at the end.
Math panel: `warp: L(2v) ≠ 2L(v)` and `slide: L(0) = [1.5; 1] ≠ 0`.

### 4. Only two arrows matter · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Because the grid stays evenly spaced, you do not need to track every vector — only the green <span class="c-i">î</span> and the red <span class="c-j">ĵ</span>. Watch where they land: î lands on [2; 1], ĵ on [−1; 1]. These two <b>landing spots</b> carry the entire grid with them.
Canvas: `morph` 0 → 1 (t∈[0, 0.6]) with the grid dimmed to 40% alpha so the two arrows stand out; pulsing rings (`g.circle`, r 0.25) appear at the landing spots (t∈[0.6, 0.8]); dashed component lines in green/red from each tip; coordinate labels "[2; 1]" (green) and "[−1; 1]" (red) appear beside the tips (t > 0.8).
Math panel: `î ↦ [2; 1]` (green) and `ĵ ↦ [−1; 1]` (red).

### 5. The matrix: landing spots as columns · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Write î's landing spot as the first column and ĵ's as the second. That 2×2 box of numbers is the <b>matrix</b> A — a complete recording of the transformation. Column one is green because it records î; column two is red because it records ĵ. Read any matrix this way.
Canvas: the two coordinate pairs fly from the arrow tips into a bracket drawn at the top-right of the canvas (`ctx.fx.fly`, t∈[0.1, 0.6]); the bracket's left column tints green, right column red (t∈[0.6, 0.8]); `highlightCol` cycles 0 → 1: highlighting a column pulses the matching arrow on canvas (t > 0.8).
Math panel: `A = [[2, −1],[1, 1]]` with column 1 green, column 2 red; the live `tex.mat` bound to `s.M`.

### 6. Why two columns are enough · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Before the motion, <span class="c-v">v</span> = 2<span class="c-i">î</span> + 1<span class="c-j">ĵ</span>: two green steps, one red step. After the motion the recipe is unchanged — only the ingredients moved. v lands on 2·(new î) + 1·(new ĵ) = [4; 2] + [−1; 1] = [3; 3]. Every vector is carried this way.
Canvas: `morph` = 0: two copies of î laid tip to tail from the origin (green), then one copy of ĵ (red) reaching v's tip (t∈[0, 0.3]). Then `morph` 0 → 1 (t∈[0.3, 0.8]): the copies become copies of the landed vectors, still tip to tail, and reach the transformed v at (3, 3); the ghost of the original v stays dashed. Labels "2·î′", "1·ĵ′" on the copies.
Math panel: `v = 2î + 1ĵ  ↦  2·[2; 1] + 1·[−1; 1] = [3; 3]`

### 7. Matrix times vector is the recipe written out · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Written out, that recipe is matrix-times-vector: A<span class="c-v">v</span> = x·(column 1) + y·(column 2). Here 2·[2; 1] + 1·[−1; 1] = [3; 3]. The first entry of v scales the green column, the second scales the red column, then add. That is all the multiplication ever does.
Canvas: the equation builds on the canvas top: the bracket A, the bracket v (yellow entries), "=" and the result bracket filling digit by digit as each term highlights (t∈[0.2, 0.8]); on the grid the two scaled columns and their sum are drawn tip to tail once more and the result tip pulses.
Math panel: `A v = 2·[2; 1] + 1·[−1; 1] = [3; 3]` with colored terms, live under `s.M` and `s.v`.

### 8. A rotation, read off the unit circle · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Rotate the grid by θ. From Part 0 you already know where <span class="c-i">î</span> lands: [cos θ; sin θ]. And <span class="c-j">ĵ</span>, a quarter turn ahead, lands on [−sin θ; cos θ]. Put them in the columns and the rotation matrix appears — nothing to memorize. At 90° it is [[0, −1],[1, 0]].
Canvas: `M` is set to rot(θ) with `theta` tweening 0 → 90° (t∈[0, 0.5]), pausing, then → 45° (t∈[0.6, 1]); the unit circle is drawn and stays a circle; î and ĵ tips slide along it with their coordinate labels updating; the matrix entries count along live.
Math panel: `R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ],[sin θ, cos θ]]` with the numeric matrix for the current θ beneath; at 90°: `[[0, −1],[1, 0]]`.

### 9. Some matrices squash · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: What if <span class="c-i">î</span> and <span class="c-j">ĵ</span> land on the same line? With A = [[1, 2],[1, 2]], both land on multiples of [1; 1], and the whole plane collapses onto that line. Many different vectors land on the same spot; information is lost. Later we measure this with the determinant and the rank.
Canvas: `M` = [[1, 2],[1, 2]]; `morph` 0 → 1 (t∈[0, 0.7]); the grid flattens onto the line y = x (drawn teal at t > 0.6); six ghost vectors with different starts converge to the same landing point and a small "lost" label appears; the lattice dots pile up on the line.
Math panel: `A = [[1, 2],[1, 2]]` with both columns highlighted as multiples of [1; 1].

### 10. Three columns in 3D · 6 s · flags: (none)
Narration: In 3D nothing changes except the count: track three arrows, record three columns. The blue <span class="c-k">k̂</span> lands in the third column. This matrix turns the cube about the vertical axis and squashes it a little in height. Orbit while it plays; the lattice stays straight and evenly spaced in every direction.
Canvas: `view` → '3d' (cam yaw 35°, pitch 25°, dist 9, auto). `g.ghostGrid()` as a range-2 cage; `g.box(lerp(I, M3, morph))` unit cube; `g.grid(M3, {plane:'all'})` lattice; î, ĵ, k̂ transformed with hats. `morph` 0 → 1 (t∈[0.1, 0.7]); auto-orbit 40° across the step.
Math panel: `M = [[1.3, −0.5, 0],[0.75, 0.87, 0],[0, 0, 0.7]]` with columns green, red, blue; `k̂ ↦ [0; 0; 0.7]`.

### 11. A learned matrix untangles data · 6 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: In machine learning a matrix is a learned transformation of your data. Two tilted, overlapping clouds are hard to split with a vertical line. Apply W — a rotation the model learned — and they become separable. Each layer of a network does this to its grid, in hundreds of dimensions, by exactly this rule.
Canvas: 2D; `twoClouds` drawn (data / dataB); a gold vertical line at x = 0 with an accuracy label "61%" (t < 0.2); `M` = W = [[0.71, 0.71],[−0.71, 0.71]] and `morph` 0 → 1 (t∈[0.2, 0.7]): the grid and the points rotate; the gold line stays; accuracy climbs to "100%"; the matrix panel is labeled W. Label "Part 2 capstone: a neural layer".
Math panel: `W = [[0.71, 0.71],[−0.71, 0.71]]` labeled "learned", with `accuracy` live.

Narration: A linear transformation moves the whole grid, keeping lines straight and evenly spaced and the origin fixed. It is fixed by where î and ĵ land; those landing spots are the columns of its matrix. Multiplying by a vector is the same recipe with new ingredients: x times column one plus y times column two.

## p2-02-matrix-vector — Matrix × vector: column view and row view
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~8 · **Prereqs** p2-01-linear-transformations, p1-06-dot-product
**Goal.** The learner computes A v two ways — as a recipe of columns and as a dot product per row — sees both give the same point, and knows the shape rule (2×2)(2×1) → (2×1).
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: h = Wx with W 128×784: 784 columns to mix, or 128 dot products to take · transfers: both views are exactly the same arithmetic in any shape; code uses whichever is faster, the picture is the same.
**Can't show.**
- 128 rows casting 128 shadows at once (we draw two and say "one per output entry")
**Terms.** column view — A v is x times column 1 plus y times column 2; row view — each entry of A v is the dot product of one row of A with v; row — one horizontal strip of a matrix; shape rule — (m×n)(n×1) gives (m×1); the inner numbers must match; elementwise product — multiplying matching entries (not what matrix multiplication does).
**State.** `M` ([[2, −1],[1, 1]]) · `v` ([2, 1]) · `viewMode` ('column' | 'row' | 'split') · `rowHighlight` (null | 0 | 1) · `colHighlight` · `shadowT` (0..1 for the row-view drops) · `wrong` (bool: show the elementwise ✕) · `tiles` (shape tiles) · `singular` (bool: switch to [[1, 2],[2, 4]])

**Steps.**
### 1. The column view, once more · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Last lesson's recipe: A<span class="c-v">v</span> is x times the green column plus y times the red column. With A = [[2, −1],[1, 1]] and v = [2; 1]: two copies of [2; 1] and one copy of [−1; 1], tip to tail, reach [3; 3]. This is the <b>column view</b>. Keep it; now we add a second way of seeing the same product.
Canvas: left half of the canvas (`viewMode = 'split'`): the two scaled columns laid tip to tail from the origin reaching (3, 3); the result in yellow; the matrix columns highlighted in turn (`colHighlight`).
Math panel: `A v = 2·[2; 1] + 1·[−1; 1] = [3; 3]` with the columns colored.

### 2. The rows are arrows too · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Now read A sideways. Its two <b>rows</b>, [2, −1] and [1, 1], are vectors as well — draw them gray. Take <span class="c-v">v</span>'s shadow on the first row arrow: the dot product row₁·v = 2·2 + (−1)·1 = 3. That number is the first entry of A v.
Canvas: right half: the two row arrows drawn gray from the origin; v drawn yellow; a dashed residual drops from v's tip onto row₁'s line and the magenta shadow times the row length gives the bar "3" (`shadowT` 0 → 1, t∈[0.2, 0.8]); the matrix panel underlines row 1.
Math panel: `(A v)₁ = row₁ · v = 2·2 + (−1)·1 = 3` with row 1 underlined.

### 3. The second row gives the second entry · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Row two does the same: row₂·v = 1·2 + 1·1 = 3, the second entry. Assemble [3; 3] from the two numbers — the same point the column view reached. One product, two stories: mix the columns, or dot each row. This is the <b>row view</b>.
Canvas: right half: the shadow onto row₂ (t∈[0, 0.5]) with bar "3"; the two bars then become the green and red components of a yellow point at (3, 3) (t∈[0.5, 0.9]); the left half's result pulses in sync; a dashed line links the two results across the split.
Math panel: `(A v)₂ = row₂ · v = 1·2 + 1·1 = 3` and `A v = [3; 3]` (both views agree).

### 4. Written out, in general · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Written out for any 2×2: [[a, b],[c, d]] [x; y] = [ax + by; cx + dy]. Read down a column: x multiplies a and c, the green column; y multiplies b and d, the red column. Read across a row: ax + by is row one dotted with v. Same formula, two highlights.
Canvas: the symbolic product drawn large across the top; `colHighlight` sweeps: the green column and x glow, then the red column and y (t < 0.5); then `rowHighlight` sweeps: row 1 and the top result entry glow, then row 2 (t > 0.5).
Math panel: `[[a, b],[c, d]] [x; y] = [ax + by; cx + dy]` with live highlighting.

### 5. Not elementwise · 3 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "multiply matching entries." That would give [2·2; 1·1] = [4; 1], a gray point far from the true [3; 3]. Matrix-times-vector always mixes: every input entry touches every output entry through the columns. Entries never pair off one to one.
Canvas: a gray point at (4, 1) labeled "[4; 1]?" appears with a dashed path from the origin; red ✕ beside it at t = 0.6; the true yellow point at (3, 3) pulses; `g.dim` between them.
Math panel: `[2·2; 1·1] = [4; 1] ≠ A v = [3; 3]`

### 6. Shapes must match · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A 2×2 matrix times a 2×1 vector gives a 2×1 vector. The inner 2s — the matrix's columns and the vector's rows — must be the same number, because each column needs an entry to scale it. Later, a 2×3 matrix eats a 3-vector and gives a 2-vector; the shape rule is the same.
Canvas: shape tiles "2×2" and "2×1" slide together; the inner 2s touch and vanish, leaving "2×1" (t < 0.5); then tiles "2×3" and "3×1" snap to "2×1" (t∈[0.5, 0.8]); a mismatched pair "2×2" and "3×1" bounces apart with a red ✕ (t > 0.85).
Math panel: `(2×2)(2×1) → (2×1) · (2×3)(3×1) → (2×1)`

### 7. A matrix is a recipe, not a picture · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Feed many vectors through A: every lattice point goes to its own A v, and together they are the moved grid of last lesson. The matrix itself is not the picture; it is the recipe that produces the picture when applied to every vector. Change one entry and every output changes.
Canvas: the full grid with lattice dots; `morph` 0 → 1 as each dot travels to its A v along a straight path; then entry a₁₂ slides −1 → 0.5 (t > 0.7) and every dot and grid line adjusts live.
Math panel: `A = [[2, a₁₂],[1, 1]]` with `a₁₂` live.

### 8. When the rows point the same way · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Switch to A = [[1, 2],[2, 4]]. Its two rows are multiples of each other, so both shadows measure the same direction and every output lands on the line y = 2x. The input [2; −1] is perpendicular to both rows: both shadows vanish and A v = 0. A non-zero input, squashed to nothing — a preview of the null space.
Canvas: `singular = true`; row arrows [1, 2] and [2, 4] drawn gray along one line; v tweens to [2, −1] (t < 0.5); both residuals drop onto the same line and the shadows shrink to a point at the origin; the output bars read 0, 0; the teal output line y = 2x appears.
Math panel: `A v = [1·2 + 2·(−1); 2·2 + 4·(−1)] = [0; 0]`

### 9. A layer is many rows at once · 5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: A layer computes h = W<span class="c-v">x</span>. Column view: mix the learned directions, one per input feature. Row view: each neuron is one row, taking its dot product with x — the neuron of p1-09, stacked. Three neurons, two inputs: a 3×2 matrix and three score bars. For a batch of inputs, the rows become the matrix X, which Part 6 shows.
Canvas: left: three gray row arrows (W = [[1, 0.5],[−0.5, 1],[0.8, −0.8]]) with v's shadows on each and three `g.bars` (scores 2.5, 0, 0.8) (t < 0.6); right: the same as a 3×2 heatmap times a 2-strip giving a 3-strip; label "p2-12: a neural layer · p6-01: batches".
Math panel: `h = W x, W: 3×2` with the three dot products.

### 10. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: A v has two faces. Column view: x times the green column plus y times the red column. Row view: each output entry is a row dotted with v. Both give the same point. Shapes chain inner-to-inner. It is never elementwise; every input touches every output.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) columns tip to tail; (2) two row arrows with shadows and bars; (3) shape tiles snapping.
Math panel: —

## p2-03-transformation-zoo — Rotation, scaling, shear, reflection, projection
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p2-01-linear-transformations
**Goal.** The learner recognizes the five basic 2D transformations from the grid, reads and writes their matrices from where î and ĵ land, and knows which preserve lengths, which flip orientation and which lose a dimension.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: standardizing 50 features is a 50×50 diagonal scaling; a rotation block of this size sits inside rotary position encodings; data augmentation rotates 28×28 images · transfers: diagonal = per-axis scaling, orthogonal = rotation/reflection, rank-deficient = projection, in every dimension; only the names "line" and "plane" change.
**Can't show.**
- a rotation in ℝⁿ as "about an axis" (n-D rotations turn planes; only 3D has an axis)

## p2-04-composition — Matrix multiplication is composition
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p2-03-transformation-zoo
**Goal.** The learner multiplies matrices by tracking î and ĵ through two maps, reads products right to left, knows order matters but grouping does not, and sees why a stack of linear layers is one matrix.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: a 3-layer network with no nonlinearity, W₃W₂W₁, collapses to a single 10×784 matrix · transfers: composition = multiplication, right-to-left order, AB ≠ BA and (AB)C = A(BC) hold for all shapes; only the number of columns tracked changes.
**Can't show.**
- a product of 128×784 and 10×128 as grid motion (we track two columns and say "one per input direction")
**Terms.** composition — doing one transformation after another; matrix product (AB) — the matrix of "B first, then A"; its columns are B's columns pushed through A; right-to-left — in AB v, the matrix nearest v acts first; commute — two maps commute if the order does not matter; most pairs do not; associative — (AB)C = A(BC): grouping does not matter; identity matrix (I) — the do-nothing matrix with 1s on the diagonal.
**State.** `R` ([[0, −1],[1, 0]]) · `S` ([[1, 1],[0, 1]]) · `stage` (0 | 1 | 2: identity, after first map, after second) · `morph` · `order` ('SR' | 'RS') · `trackI`, `trackJ` (0..1 hop progress) · `C` (third matrix diag(1.5, 1) for associativity) · `tiles` (shape tiles) · `layers` (three matrices for the ML step) · `v` ([1, 1]) · `B` (check 1 editable)

**Steps.**
### 1. Rotate, then shear · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two transformations, one after another. First a quarter-turn rotation R = [[0, −1],[1, 0]]. Pause. Then the shear S = [[1, 1],[0, 1]]. Each stage leaves a ghost so you can see where the grid has been. The whole trip is called the <b>composition</b> of the two maps.
Canvas: `stage` 0 → 1: grid morphs under R (t∈[0, 0.4]) over the dashed ghost; a second, dotted ghost is left at the rotated state; `stage` 1 → 2: the grid morphs under S applied to the rotated grid (t∈[0.55, 0.95]); î, ĵ ride along with hats.
Math panel: `R = [[0, −1],[1, 0]]` then `S = [[1, 1],[0, 1]]`, each lit as it acts.

### 2. The result is linear, so it has a matrix · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Look at the end state: lines are still straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin has not moved. The trip is itself a linear transformation — so it must have its own matrix. Which one? Watch the grid go directly from start to finish; the panel shows the unknown entries as question marks.
Canvas: the grid resets to the ghost and morphs directly to the final state in one motion (t∈[0.1, 0.8]); the matrix panel on canvas shows "[[?, ?],[?, ?]]".
Math panel: `SR = [[?, ?],[?, ?]]`

### 3. Track î through both hops · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Follow <span class="c-i">î</span>. The rotation sends it to [0; 1]. The shear then sends [0; 1] to [1; 1]. So the whole trip sends î to [1; 1] — that is the first column of the product. Two hops, one landing spot.
Canvas: `trackI` 0 → 1: a green path with an intermediate ghost arrow at [0, 1] (hop 1, t∈[0, 0.4]) and the final green arrow at [1, 1] (hop 2, t∈[0.4, 0.8]); the pair "[1; 1]" flies into column 1 of the product bracket (t > 0.8).
Math panel: `î → [0; 1] → [1; 1]` and `SR = [[1, ?],[1, ?]]`

### 4. Track ĵ · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Now <span class="c-j">ĵ</span>. The rotation sends it to [−1; 0]. The shear leaves [−1; 0] alone, because it only leans vertical things. So ĵ lands on [−1; 0]: the second column. The product is complete: SR = [[1, −1],[1, 0]].
Canvas: `trackJ` 0 → 1: red path with the intermediate ghost at [−1, 0] (which does not move under S — a small "stays" label); the pair flies into column 2.
Math panel: `ĵ → [−1; 0] → [−1; 0]` and `SR = [[1, −1],[1, 0]]`

### 5. Read right to left · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: In symbols, "rotate then shear" is S(R<span class="c-v">v</span>) = (SR)<span class="c-v">v</span>. Read it <b>right to left</b>, like f(g(x)): the matrix nearest the vector acts first. Writing SR for "R first" feels backwards at first; the picture of v hopping twice makes it natural.
Canvas: the yellow v = [1, 1] hops: first R v = [−1, 1] (ghost), then S(R v) = [0, 1]; under the equation on canvas, an arrow labeled "first" curves under R and "second" under S.
Math panel: `S(R v) = (SR) v` with "first" under R and "second" under S; `v = [1; 1] → [−1; 1] → [0; 1]`.

### 6. The product, column by column · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Matrix multiplication is defined so that this works. Column j of SR is column j of R pushed through S — the matrix-times-vector recipe from p2-02. Column 1 of R is [0; 1]: S·[0; 1] = 0·[1; 0] + 1·[1; 1] = [1; 1]. Column 2 of R is [−1; 0]: S·[−1; 0] = [−1; 0].
Canvas: the green column of R is highlighted and fed into S with the tip-to-tail recipe (0 copies of S's col 1, 1 copy of S's col 2) landing on [1, 1] (t < 0.5); then the red column (t∈[0.5, 0.9]); each lands in the product bracket.
Math panel: `SR = [S·col₁(R)  S·col₂(R)] = [[1, −1],[1, 0]]`

### 7. Entry by entry: row dot column · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Entry by entry, each number of the product is a dot product: row i of the left matrix with column j of the right. Row 1 of S is [1, 1]; column 1 of R is [0; 1]; their dot product is 1 — the top-left entry. Four such dot products fill the product.
Canvas: a highlight bar slides along row 1 of S and down column 1 of R; the two pairs of numbers multiply and add to "1" which drops into position (1, 1); the other three entries fill the same way (0.6 s each).
Math panel: `(SR)ᵢⱼ = rowᵢ(S) · colⱼ(R)` with each entry's sum shown as it fills.

### 8. Order matters · 5 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "AB = BA." Try the other order: shear first, then rotate. The grids end in different places, and the matrices differ: RS = [[0, −1],[1, 1]], not SR = [[1, −1],[1, 0]]. Transformations rarely <b>commute</b>. The order you write is the reverse of the order you do.
Canvas: split screen: left plays R then S (`order = 'SR'`), right plays S then R (`order = 'RS'`), simultaneously (t∈[0, 0.8]); the final grids differ visibly; a red ✕ on the label "SR = RS" at t = 0.85 with the two matrices beneath.
Math panel: `SR = [[1, −1],[1, 0]] ≠ RS = [[0, −1],[1, 1]]`

### 9. Grouping does not matter · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Three maps in a row: C, then R, then S. Whether you think of it as (SR)C or S(RC), it is the same sequence of motions — nothing about the trip changes when you bracket it differently. Matrix multiplication is <b>associative</b>; the order of the factors is fixed, the grouping is free.

Math panel: `(SR)C = S(RC)` with brackets sliding.

### 10. Shapes must chain · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A product only makes sense if the first map's output size is the next map's input size. Shape tiles: (2×3)(3×2) chains, the inner 3s meet, and the result is 2×2. (2×3)(2×3) does not chain: the inner numbers differ and the tiles bounce apart.

Math panel: `(2×3)(3×2) → (2×2) · (2×3)(2×3) ✕`

### 11. A stack of linear layers is one matrix · 6 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: A network with three linear layers and nothing between them computes W₃W₂W₁<span class="c-v">x</span>. That product is a single matrix: three grid motions collapse into one. ### 12. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: Multiplying matrices composes transformations; read right to left. The columns of AB are B's columns pushed through A; each entry is a row dotted with a column. Order matters, grouping does not, and shapes must chain. A stack of linear layers is one matrix.

## p2-06-determinant — Determinant: area, volume, orientation (+ cross product)
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~12 · **Prereqs** p2-04-composition, p2-05-3d-transformations
**Goal.** The learner reads the determinant as the factor by which areas (volumes) are scaled, with sign for orientation and zero for collapse, derives ad − bc from a picture, and meets the cross product as the area-vector of a 3D parallelogram.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: det of a 784×784 Jacobian appears in normalizing flows; det of a 50×50 covariance in a Gaussian likelihood · transfers: "volume factor, sign = orientation, zero = collapse" is exact for n×n matrices; the cross product exists only in 3D (its n-D job is done by the determinant).
**Can't show.**
- a 784-dimensional volume (it is computed; the picture is the 3D box)
- a cross product in ℝ⁴ (there is none; we say so)
**Terms.** determinant (det A) — the factor by which A multiplies every area (2D) or volume (3D), negative when orientation flips, zero when space is flattened; orientation — whether î-then-ĵ turns counter-clockwise (positive) or clockwise (negative); singular — det = 0: the map flattens space and loses information; cross product (u × v) — a 3D vector perpendicular to both u and v whose length is the area of their parallelogram; right-hand rule — the direction of u × v: curl fingers from u to v, the thumb points along u × v.
**State.** `M` (2×2) · `morph` · `fill` ('pos' | 'neg' by sign) · `areaLabel` (live |det|) · `showTiles` · `dissect` (0..1 for the ad − bc dissection) · `M3` (3×3) · `u`, `v` (3D vectors [2, 0, 1], [0, 1.5, 0.5]) · `cross` (computed) · `flipOrder` (bool) · `w` (third vector for the triple product) · `cloud` (density demo) · `view` · `cam` · `guess` (entry)

**Steps.**
### 1. The unit square under a stretch · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Every linear transformation stretches or squashes areas. Watch the unit square — the square with sides <span class="c-i">î</span> and <span class="c-j">ĵ</span>, area 1. Under A = diag(3, 2) it becomes a 3×2 rectangle. Its area counter climbs from 1 to 6.

Math panel: `A = [[3, 0],[0, 2]]`, `area = 6`

### 2. The determinant is that factor · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The number by which area is multiplied is the <b>determinant</b>: det A = 6, a 3× stretch times a 2× stretch. Six copies of the unit square tile the rectangle exactly. One number summarizes how much the map inflates or deflates everything.
Canvas: six unit-square tiles fade in to fill the rectangle one by one (`showTiles`, 0.3 s each) with a running count.
Math panel: `det A = 3 · 2 = 6`

### 3. Every region scales by the same factor · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Not only the unit square — any region is scaled by det A. A blob is made of tiny grid squares, and the grid is evenly spaced, so every tiny square scales identically. Area 2.1 becomes 12.6. The determinant is a property of the map, not of the shape.
Canvas: a circular blob (`g.polygon`, 64 vertices, radius 0.82, area ≈ 2.1) tiled by small squares (`g.grid` step 0.25 clipped to the blob); `morph` 0 → 1; the area label ×6.
Math panel: `area(A·region) = det A · area(region)`

### 4. A shear changes no area · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The shear [[1, 1],[0, 1]] leans the square into a parallelogram but keeps its base and its height, so the area stays 1: det = 1. Leaning is free; only stretching costs area. Rotations also have det = 1 — they move the square without changing it.

Math panel: `det [[1, 1],[0, 1]] = 1 · det R(θ) = 1`

### 5. ad − bc is the picture · 6 s · flags: (none)
Narration: For A = [[a, b],[c, d]] the formula is det A = ad − bc. Here is where it comes from. Enclose the parallelogram in a rectangle of sides a + b and c + d. Subtract the two small rectangles (bc each) and the four triangles; what remains is ad − bc. With A = [[2, 1],[1, 3]]: 6 − 1 = 5. Watch once; you will not need to memorize it.
Canvas: `M` = [[2, 1],[1, 3]]; `dissect` 0 → 1: the enclosing (a+b)×(c+d) = 3×4 rectangle outlines (t < 0.2); the two bc rectangles (1×1) shade and subtract with labels "bc" (t∈[0.2, 0.5]); the four triangles pair into two more rectangles (ac and bd) and subtract (t∈[0.5, 0.85]); the parallelogram remains with "ad − bc = 5".
Math panel: `(a+b)(c+d) − 2bc − ac − bd = ad − bc = 2·3 − 1·1 = 5`

Narration: Belief: "area is never negative, so the determinant is never negative." Swing <span class="c-j">ĵ</span> clockwise past <span class="c-i">î</span>. Narration: The special moment is det = 0: <span class="c-j">ĵ</span> lies on <span class="c-i">î</span>'s line and the square is squashed flat onto a line. ### 9. The cross product: an area as an arrow · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two 3D vectors, <span class="c-v">u</span> = [2; 0; 1] and <span class="c-w">v</span> = [0; 1.5; 0.5], span a parallelogram of area 3.5. ### 10. Order flips the cross product · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Swap the order and the arrow flips: v × u = −(u × v). The <b>right-hand rule</b> says which way: curl your fingers from the first vector to the second and your thumb points along the product. ### 11. Cross product meets determinant · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Add a third vector <span class="c-b">w</span>. The volume of the box spanned by u, v, w is (u × v)·w: the parallelogram's area times w's height above it. That is exactly det[u v w]. ### 12. Determinants multiply · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Do one map, then another: areas scale by the first determinant, then by the second. So det(AB) = det(A)·det(B). Scale by 2 and then by 3 and you have scaled by 6. Consequence: if any factor has det 0, the whole composition is singular.

## p2-07-inverse-solving — Inverse: running the map backwards; solving Ax = b
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~9 · **Prereqs** p2-06-determinant
**Goal.** The learner sees A⁻¹ as the movie played backwards, uses it to solve Ax = b by dragging b home, knows the 2×2 formula and why det = 0 forbids an inverse, and learns the ML habit "solve, don't invert".

Narration: For a 2×2 there is a short formula: A<sup>−1</sup> = (1/det A)·[[d, −b],[−c, a]]. Swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant. Narration: The identity I = [[1, 0],[0, 1]] leaves every vector alone: AI = IA = A. ## p2-08-rank-column-null — Rank, column space, null space
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p2-07-inverse-solving, p1-03-linear-combinations-span
**Goal.** The learner sees the column space as the teal set of all outputs, the null space as the magenta set of inputs sent to zero, rank as the surviving dimension, and reads "3 = 2 + 1" from a flattened 3D space.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: a 784×128 layer has rank ≤ 128; a LoRA update has rank 4–64; a data matrix with duplicated features has rank below its column count · transfers: rank + nullity = number of inputs for every matrix; column space = span of columns = all reachable outputs, in any dimension.
**Can't show.**

**Terms.** column space — the span of the columns: every output the matrix can produce; rank — the dimension of the column space: how many directions survive; null space — all inputs the matrix sends to the zero vector; nullity — the dimension of the null space: how many input directions are lost; rank–nullity — rank + nullity = number of input dimensions.

### 1. The column space: everything the map can output · 4 s · flags: (none)

## p2-10-transpose-outer-symmetric — Transpose, xᵀy, outer products, symmetric matrices

**Goal.** The learner flips a matrix across its diagonal, writes the dot product as a row times a column, sees the outer product build a rank-1 matrix, and recognizes symmetric matrices as the ones that stretch along perpendicular axes.

### 1. Flip across the diagonal · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The <b>transpose</b> Aᵀ flips a matrix across its diagonal: rows become columns. For B = [[1, 2, 3],[4, 5, 6]], a 2×3, the transpose is 3×2: the first row [1, 2, 3] stands up as the first column. The diagonal entries do not move; every other entry trades places with its mirror.

### 2. A vector lying down · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A column vector transposed is a <b>row vector</b>, written lying down: xᵀ = [1 1]. Same numbers, sideways. We will need lying-down vectors for exactly one thing: putting a row next to a column.

### 3. Row times column is the dot product · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Put the row xᵀ in front of the column y and multiply as a (1×2)(2×1) product: one row dotted with one column — a 1×1 result, the dot product x·y = 1·1 + 1·0 = 1. Writing x·y as xᵀy is the habit of every ML paper, and the atom of every matrix product.

### 4. Aᵀ moves the matrix to the other side · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Here is why the transpose matters: (A<span class="c-v">x</span>)·y = <span class="c-v">x</span>·(Aᵀy). Transform x and measure against y, or transform y by Aᵀ and measure against x — the same number. With A = [[2, 1],[0, 3]], x = [1; 1], y = [1; 0]: Ax = [3; 3], (Ax)·y = 3; Aᵀy = [2; 1], x·(Aᵀy) = 3. ### 5. The transpose is not the inverse · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "Aᵀ undoes A." For A = [[2, 1],[0, 3]], apply Aᵀ after A: AᵀA = [[4, 2],[2, 10]], nowhere near the identity, and the grid does not return to the ghost. Narration: (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ: flip a product and the factors swap order, each transposed. The shape tiles show why: (2×3)(3×4) is 2×4; its transpose is 4×2, which only chains as (4×3)(3×2) = BᵀAᵀ. Order reverses because inputs and outputs trade places.

### 7. Column times row: the outer product · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Reverse the dot product's shape: a column times a row, (2×1)(1×2), is a 2×2 matrix — the <b>outer product</b> uvᵀ. With u = [1; 2], v = [3; 1]: [[3, 1],[6, 2]]. Each cell is uᵢvⱼ; each column is a multiple of u. As a map it collapses the plane onto the line of u: rank 1.

### 8. Symmetric matrices: perpendicular stretch axes · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A matrix equal to its own transpose, aᵢⱼ = aⱼᵢ, is <b>symmetric</b>. ## p2-11-affine-bias — Affine maps: Wx + b and the homogeneous trick
**Part** 2 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~9 · **Prereqs** p2-01-linear-transformations, p2-05-3d-transformations

## p2-12-neural-layer — A neural layer: transformation plus a fold
**Part** 2 · **Tags** capstone · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~14 · **Prereqs** p2-09-non-square, p2-11-affine-bias, p1-09-first-model-neuron, p2-04-composition
**Goal.** The learner watches a width-3 layer lift the plane into an exactly 3D hidden space, bend it with tanh, and cut it with a gold plane whose preimage is a curved boundary around the inner circle — and sees why a stack of linear layers could never do this.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D input, exactly 3D hidden space (width 3), 2D output; the spirals' 8-D hidden space is shown as a labeled PCA-3 projection · realSize: hidden widths 256–4096, dozens of layers; W₁ is 128×784 in a digit classifier · transfers: "affine lift, elementwise fold, flat cut" is the exact mechanism at every width and depth; only the hidden space stops being drawable above 3.
**Can't show.**
- the 8-dimensional hidden space of the spirals network (we show a PCA-3 shadow and print the variance kept)
- that in very high dimensions almost every labeled point set is already linearly separable (Cover's result); the lift is still the mechanism, but the "circles need a lift" picture does not mean "everything needs one"
**Terms.** hidden layer — the intermediate vector h = fold(W₁x + b₁); its size is the width; width — the number of hidden units: the dimension of the hidden space; activation (fold) — a fixed nonlinear function applied to each coordinate separately, such as tanh or ReLU; tanh — squashes any number smoothly into (−1, 1); ReLU — replaces negatives by 0, leaves positives alone: a fold along each axis; decision boundary (of a network) — the input points whose output score is 0: the preimage of the gold plane; preimage — all inputs that land on a given output set; depth — the number of layers composed.
**State.** `dataset` ('circles' | 'spirals') · `X`, `labels` (`circles120` or `spirals200`) · `act` ('tanh' | 'relu' | 'none') · `W1` (3×2), `b1` (3), `w2` (3), `b2` (scalar) — width-3 weights · `W8` ({W1 8×2, b1, W2 8×8, b2, w3, b3}) hard-coded spirals weights · `stage` (0..3 continuous: 0 input, 1 after affine, 2 after fold, 3 output) · `trainHist` (snapshots every 25 steps of the width-3 training) · `trainIdx` · `frozenUnit` (null | 0 | 1 | 2) · `pca3` ({components, varianceKept}) for spirals · `planeHandle` (normal w2 and offset for check 2) · `probe` · `view` · `cam`
Hard-coded verified weights (used as defaults and as the fallback if in-browser training does not reach 98% accuracy): tanh circles: W1 rows [2, 0], [−1, 1.732], [−1, −1.732]; b1 = [1.5, 1.5, 1.5]; w2 = [1, 1, 1]; b2 = −1.5 (output score = w2·h + b2, positive = inner class; boundary radius ≈ 1.0–1.25 in every direction, margins ≥ 0.3 on both circles). ReLU circles: W1 rows [1, 0], [−0.5, 0.866], [−0.5, −0.866]; b1 = [0.3, 0.3, 0.3]; w2 = [−1, −1, −1]; b2 = 1.45 (positive = inner; hexagon-like boundary at radius ≈ 1.0–1.15). "none": any weights — the picture shows the best line, accuracy ≈ 50%. In-browser training (the only model trained at load, per the plan): width 3, tanh, init `LA.rng(12)` uniform ±1, full-batch gradient descent on mean squared error of tanh(score) against ±1 labels, learning rate 0.1, 1200 steps (≤ 150 ms), snapshot every 25 steps into `trainHist`; if final accuracy < 98%, use the hard-coded weights and label "preset weights". Spirals: width 8, 2 → 8 → 8 → 1 tanh, weights hard-coded in the lesson file (obtained once offline with the same trainer, 4000 steps, lr 0.05, seed 13), asserted ≥ 95% accuracy at load; an optional "Retrain (4000 steps)" button.

**Steps.**
### 1. A line cannot do it · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two classes: a blue inner ring and a rose outer ring. A neuron from Part 1 draws a straight boundary; sweep it anywhere you like and about half the points are wrong. No line separates a ring from the ring around it. We need a map that bends space before the cut.
Canvas: 2D range 2.5; `circles120` drawn (data / dataB); a gold line sweeps through angles and offsets (t < 0.8) with a live accuracy label hovering around 50%; red ✕ beside "straight boundary" at t = 0.85.
Math panel: `best line: accuracy ≈ 50%`

### 2. Stacking linear layers does not help · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "stack several layers of W and the boundary will curve." From p2-04, W₂W₁ is a single matrix, and a straight boundary stays straight through it. Three grid morphs collapse into one, and the gold cut is still a line. Depth alone adds nothing; the fold is what matters.
Canvas: the grid plays W₁ (shear), W₂ (rotation), W₃ (scale) in sequence (t < 0.5), then collapses to one direct morph (t∈[0.5, 0.8]); the gold line is carried along and stays straight; red ✕ beside "W₂W₁ bends" at t = 0.85.
Math panel: `W₂W₁x = (W₂W₁)x` · `accuracy ≈ 50%`

### 3. Layer 1: lift the plane into 3D · 6 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The first layer is affine with three outputs: W₁ is 3×2 and b₁ has three entries. From p2-09 and p2-11, that lifts the plane into 3D as a tilted sheet — and the 120 points ride along, still on that flat sheet. This is the <b>hidden layer</b>, width 3, so the hidden space is exactly three-dimensional. Orbit.
Canvas: view → 3D (cam yaw 35°, pitch 25°, dist 7, auto-orbit 40°); `stage` 0 → 1: the 2D grid and points fly to W₁x + b₁ on a tilted plane (`g.planes` translucent; `g.surface` parametric (x, y) ↦ W₁[x; y] + b₁ as a grid); the three hidden axes h₁, h₂, h₃ drawn in green, red, blue.
Math panel: `h_pre = W₁x + b₁` with `W₁ = [[2, 0],[−1, 1.73],[−1, −1.73]]`, `b₁ = [1.5; 1.5; 1.5]`.

### 4. The fold: tanh bends the sheet · 6 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Now the <b>activation</b>: tanh applied to each of the three coordinates separately. Numbers far from zero are squashed toward ±1, so the flat sheet bends into a curved surface inside the cube [−1, 1]³. Watch the inner ring gather on one side of the bend while the outer ring is pushed toward the cube's walls.
Canvas: `stage` 1 → 2: the sheet morphs vertex by vertex from W₁x + b₁ to tanh(W₁x + b₁) (`g.surface` parametric, res 30, translucent, wire); the points follow; the unit cube drawn as a faint cage; a small inset shows the tanh curve.
Math panel: `h = tanh(W₁x + b₁)` with the tanh graph icon.

### 5. Three neurons, three directions · 5 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Each hidden unit is a neuron from p1-09: a weight row of W₁ with a bias, a dot product and a fold. Its weight row is one direction in the input plane and one axis of the hidden cube. Freeze a unit — set its output to a constant — and the sheet flattens along that axis. Three directions at 120° surround the inner ring.
Canvas: the three weight rows drawn as green, red, blue arrows in a 2D inset of the input plane (at 0°, 120°, 240°); toggling `frozenUnit` (interactive buttons) flattens the sheet along that axis (t: unit 0 freezes at t∈[0.3, 0.6], restores after); the corresponding hidden axis dims.
Math panel: `hᵢ = tanh(wᵢ·x + bᵢ), i = 1, 2, 3`

### 6. Layer 2: a flat cut in hidden space · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The output layer is a single neuron on the hidden vector: score = w₂·h + b₂, with w₂ = [1; 1; 1] and b₂ = −1.5. Its boundary is the gold plane h₁ + h₂ + h₃ = 1.5 in the hidden cube — flat, as every neuron's boundary is. It slices the bent sheet: inner points on one side, outer points on the other. 100% correct.
Canvas: the gold plane (`g.planes`, normal [1, 1, 1], offset 1.5/√3) fades in and clips against the cube; points colored by side; the accuracy label climbs to 100%; the plane's normal drawn as a gold arrow.
Math panel: `score = w₂·h + b₂ = h₁ + h₂ + h₃ − 1.5`

### 7. Back in the plane: a curved boundary · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Pull the cut back to the input plane. The points of the plane that land exactly on the gold plane form a closed curve around the inner ring — the <b>decision boundary</b>, the <b>preimage</b> of a flat cut. The network never drew a curve; it bent space until a flat cut did the work. The warped grid shows how the network sees the plane.

### 11. ReLU folds along axes · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Swap tanh for <b>ReLU</b>, which sets negatives to zero.
