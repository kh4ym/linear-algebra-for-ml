# Part 4 — Change of basis, eigenvectors, SVD, PCA

**Through-line.** Part 4 is about finding the *right eyes* for a matrix. Change of basis shows that the same arrow and the same motion can be written in many coordinate systems; eigenvectors are the directions a matrix merely stretches; in an eigenbasis the matrix is diagonal and its powers are trivial (power iteration, Markov chains, PageRank). Symmetric matrices have perpendicular eigenvectors and draw bowls and saddles; SVD extends "rotate–stretch–rotate" to every matrix, giving low-rank compression, the pseudo-inverse and the condition number. The covariance matrix turns a data cloud into an ellipse, PCA reads the ellipse's axes, and whitening/Mahalanobis use them to measure distance in the data's own units.

**Order (ids final).** p4-01-change-of-basis → p4-02-eigenvectors → p4-03-diagonalization-powers → p4-04-simplex-softmax → p4-05-markov-pagerank ★ → p4-06-symmetric-quadratic-forms → p4-07-svd → p4-08-low-rank-compression ★ → p4-09-covariance → p4-10-pca ★ → p4-11-whitening-mahalanobis ★ → p4-12-pseudoinverse-conditioning. The simplex/softmax lesson is deliberately placed *before* Markov so that "a stochastic matrix maps the triangle into itself ⇒ a fixed point with λ = 1" is seen, not asserted.

**Shared numbers.** The workhorse symmetric matrix is A = [[2,1],[1,2]] (eigenvalues 3 and 1, eigenvectors [1;1] and [1;−1]); the non-symmetric example is A = [[3,1],[0,2]] (eigenvalues 3 and 2, eigenvectors [1;0] and [1;−1]); the SVD example is A = [[2,1],[0,1.5]] (σ₁ ≈ 2.38, σ₂ ≈ 1.26); the weather chain is P = [[0.8,0.1,0.2],[0.1,0.7,0.3],[0.1,0.2,0.5]] (columns sum to 1, stationary π ≈ [0.41, 0.36, 0.23], other eigenvalues 0.67 and 0.33); the covariance cloud is LA.rng.gaussianCloud(seed 49, n 80, {mean:[2,1], cov:[[2,1.2],[1.2,1]]}). Eigenvectors, singular vectors and principal components are always **gold** (`c-gold`) with letter labels; the ghost grid is dim gray; projections magenta with dashed gray residuals.

## p4-01-change-of-basis — Same vector, different coordinates
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p1-03-linear-combinations-span, p2-07-inverse-solving, p2-04-composition
**Goal.** The learner can translate a vector's coordinates between two bases with B and B⁻¹, write a transformation in another basis as B⁻¹AB, and see that the eigenbasis makes a matrix diagonal.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: a 768-dimensional hidden layer "rotated" into PCA coordinates is B⁻¹AB with a 768×768 B · transfers: B converts new → old, B⁻¹ old → new, B⁻¹AB is the same map in new coordinates, in any dimension.
**Can't show.**
- Bases of more than three vectors; the two-grid overlay is strictly 2D (3D would need three skewed grids).
- Non-uniqueness of basis choice beyond two examples.
**Terms.** basis — a set of arrows every vector can be written from in exactly one way; coordinates — the recipe numbers for a vector in a given basis; change-of-basis matrix B — columns are the new basis vectors written in the old coordinates; similar matrices — A and B⁻¹AB: the same transformation seen in two bases; eigenbasis — a basis of eigenvectors, in which the transformation is diagonal.
**State.** `s.b1`, `s.b2` (Alice's basis vectors, purple; default [2;1], [−1;1]), `s.v` (yellow vector, default [1;2] in our coordinates), `s.B` (derived), `s.showOurGrid`, `s.showHerGrid` (bools), `s.A` (transformation to view: default rot 90°), `s.stage` (0..3 for the three-tween B⁻¹AB path), `s.t`, `s.eigenMode` (bool), `s.gridTween` (0..1 for the B⁻¹ straightening).
**Steps.**
### 1. Two people, one arrow · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two people describe the same yellow arrow. We use <span class="c-i">î</span> and <span class="c-j">ĵ</span> and say <span class="c-v">v</span> = [1; 2]. Alice uses her own arrows <span class="c-w">b₁</span> = [2; 1] and <span class="c-w">b₂</span> = [−1; 1] and says [1; 1]. Same arrow, different numbers — because different rulers.
Canvas: Our gray grid; Alice's grid (g.grid(B) in purple at 35 % alpha) fades in over it (t 0→0.5); b₁, b₂ purple with labels; v yellow. Two readouts beside v's tip: "us: [1; 2]" (white) and "Alice: [1; 1]" (purple).
Math panel: `v = 1·î + 2·ĵ`, `v = 1·b₁ + 1·b₂`.
### 2. Alice's recipe · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Alice's [1; 1] means one step along <span class="c-w">b₁</span>, one step along <span class="c-w">b₂</span>. Tip to tail: [2; 1] + [−1; 1] = [1; 2]. Her coordinates are a recipe; her basis vectors are the ingredients; our coordinates are the result.
Canvas: One purple copy of b₁ from the origin, then b₂ from its tip, landing on v's tip with a gold ring. The purple grid lines through the path highlight.
Math panel: `1·[2;1] + 1·[−1;1] = [1;2]`.
### 3. B translates Alice → us · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: That recipe is a matrix–vector product. Put her basis vectors as columns: <b>B</b> = [b₁ b₂] = [[2,−1],[1,1]]. Then <i>our</i> coordinates = B · (<i>her</i> coordinates). B is a dictionary from Alice's words to ours.
Canvas: b₁ and b₂ fly (ctx.fx.fly) into the two columns of B in the panel (both purple, labeled b₁, b₂ above the columns); the product B·[1;1] computes with colored terms and equals [1;2], which flies back to v's tip.
Math panel: `B = [[2,−1],[1,1]]`, `B·[1;1] = [1;2]`.
### 4. B⁻¹ translates us → Alice · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: To go the other way, run the dictionary backwards: her coordinates = <b>B⁻¹</b> · our coordinates. Watch what B⁻¹ does to the picture: it straightens Alice's skewed grid into the ordinary square grid, and <span class="c-v">v</span> lands on [1; 1] in that square grid.
Canvas: s.gridTween 0→1: the whole scene (both grids, v) is transformed by lerp(I, B⁻¹): Alice's purple grid becomes the standard square grid while our gray grid skews; v's tip moves to [1,1]; readout "[1; 1]". Then it eases back (t 0.7→1) to show it was only a change of description.
Math panel: `B⁻¹ = (1/3)[[1,1],[−1,2]]`, `B⁻¹·[1;2] = [1;1]`.
### 5. Misconception: changing basis moves the arrow · 3 s · flags: misconception
Narration: ✕ "Multiplying by B moves <span class="c-v">v</span> to a new place." No: the arrow never moves. B changes which <i>numbers</i> we attach to it. Apply B to the arrow itself and you get a different arrow — the wrong idea, marked in red. The right picture: same arrow, two labels.
Canvas: A red dashed copy of v is transformed by B to Bv = [0,3] with a red g.marker.cross; it fades. v stays put with its two readouts and a green g.marker.check.
Math panel: `v (fixed)`, `coords_us = B · coords_Alice`.
### 6. A transformation in Alice's words: three tweens · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: We write a quarter-turn as <b>R</b> = [[0,−1],[1,0]]. How does Alice write the same motion? Take her coordinates to ours (B), rotate (R), translate back (B⁻¹). Three moves, one matrix: <b>B⁻¹RB</b> = [[0.33,−0.67],[1.67,−0.33]]. Strange numbers, same motion.
Canvas: s.stage 0→3: (1) scene transformed by B (Alice's grid becomes square — her view), (2) rotate 90° (tween the angle), (3) transform by B⁻¹ back to our view. The three panel tiles light in turn; v is tracked through all three hops with ghosts.
Math panel: `A_Alice = B⁻¹ R B` with the three tiles, then the product.
### 7. Same motion, different eyes: what stays the same · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Different numbers, same motion — so anything that is <i>about the motion</i> must agree: the determinant (area factor) is 1 in both, and the eigenvalues are the same. Matrices related by B⁻¹AB are called <b>similar</b>; they are one transformation in two languages.
Canvas: Both grids rotate together by 90° (one tween); two readouts "det = 1.00" in white and purple; eigenvalue readouts match ("complex, |λ| = 1" here).
Math panel: `det(B⁻¹AB) = det A`, `eig(B⁻¹AB) = eig A`.
### 8. The best eyes: an eigenbasis makes A diagonal · 4.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Now A = [[2,1],[1,2]]. Choose Alice's arrows to be the gold directions A merely stretches: <span class="c-gold">p₁</span> = [1;1] (×3) and <span class="c-gold">p₂</span> = [1;−1] (×1). In that basis the motion is pure stretching: <b>P⁻¹AP</b> = [[3,0],[0,1]], a diagonal matrix. No shear, no turning — just two numbers.
Canvas: s.eigenMode: b₁,b₂ tween to [1,1], [1,−1] and turn gold; the gold grid (g.grid(P)) morphs under A: every grid cell stretches 3× along p₁ and 1× along p₂ with no shear, while the gray grid under A visibly shears. Panel D fills.
Math panel: `P = [[1,1],[1,−1]]`, `D = P⁻¹AP = [[3,0],[0,1]]`.
### 9. Why ML cares: coordinates are a choice · 3.5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: PCA is exactly this: rotate the data into the basis where its spread is along the axes. An embedding space is only meaningful up to a change of basis — rotate every word vector together and nothing changes. The picture is 2D; the operation is two matrix multiplications in any dimension.
Canvas: A tilted cloud of 40 points (LA.rng.gaussianCloud seed 41) rotates into a gold axis-aligned frame; right: a 6×6 heatmap of B⁻¹AB computed for a 6×6 case labeled "same map, new coordinates — 6 dims". Caption: "The picture stops at 3. The formula does not."
Math panel: `A_new = B⁻¹ A B`.
### 10. Recap · 2.5 s · flags: recap
Narration: Columns of B are the new basis in old coordinates; B converts new → old, B⁻¹ old → new. The arrow never moves; its numbers do. A transformation in the new basis is B⁻¹AB; in an eigenbasis it is diagonal.
Canvas: Three mini-cards: (1) one yellow arrow with two readouts over two grids; (2) three tiles B⁻¹·A·B; (3) a gold grid stretching with D = diag(3,1).
Math panel: `B⁻¹AB`.
**Playground.** controls: handle · bind `b1` · drag · "b₁"; handle · bind `b2` · drag · "b₂"; handle · bind `v` · drag · "v"; toggle · bind `showHerGrid` · "Alice's grid"; preset · bind `A` · "rotation 90° / shear / A = [[2,1],[1,2]]" · "transformation"; button · "Play B⁻¹AB" (replays the three tweens). **Checks.**

## p4-02-eigenvectors — Eigenvectors and eigenvalues
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~12 · **Prereqs** p4-01-change-of-basis, p2-06-determinant, p2-05-3d-transformations
**Goal.** The learner can spot the directions a matrix only stretches, name the stretch factors, find them by sliding λ until A − λI collapses the grid, and knows that rotations have no real eigenvectors (an axis exists only in 3D) while symmetric matrices have perpendicular ones.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: the covariance of 784-pixel images has 784 eigenvectors; a 4096×4096 recurrent weight has 4096 eigenvalues deciding whether signals explode · transfers: Av = λv and det(A − λI) = 0 are the definition in any size; "every rotation has an axis" is true only in 3D.
**Can't show.**
- Complex eigenvalues as arrows (we show the det bar never touching zero and the spiral they cause).
- Repeated eigenvalues with too few eigenvectors (a shear in n-D) beyond the 2D shear case.
- Eigenvectors of a 4×4 or larger matrix as arrows.
**Terms.** eigenvector — a nonzero vector that a matrix keeps on its own line, only stretched or flipped; eigenvalue λ — the stretch factor on that line (negative = flipped); characteristic equation — det(A − λI) = 0, the condition that A − λI squashes something to zero; eigenline — the whole line of eigenvectors through the origin; real vs complex eigenvalues — real when some direction is preserved, complex when every direction turns.
**State.** `s.A` (2×2, default [[3,1],[0,2]]), `s.fan` (24 unit vectors, drawn gray; after transform, their images), `s.t` (morph), `s.lambda` (slider value), `s.showDetBar` (bool), `s.eig` (derived via LA.num.eig2 with complex flag; stabilized), `s.v` (yellow test vector), `s.mode` ('shear'|'rotation'|'spiral'|'symmetric'|'3d'), `s.view`, `s.cam`, `s.axis3` (3D rotation axis), `s.theta3`, `s.powerK` (0..6).
**Steps.**
### 1. Most vectors are knocked off their line · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Apply <b>A</b> = [[3,1],[0,2]] to a fan of 24 unit arrows. Nearly every arrow turns as well as stretches — it leaves the line it started on. The yellow <span class="c-v">v</span> = [1; 2] swings to [5; 4]: a different direction entirely.
Canvas: Fan of 24 gray unit vectors; grid morph I → A over the ghost grid (t 0→1); each arrow's original left as a dashed ghost; v yellow with its ghost; a thin gray g.line through v's ghost shows the line it left.
Math panel: `A = [[3,1],[0,2]]`, `Av = [5;4]`.
### 2. Some stay put: the eigenvectors · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two directions do not turn. <span class="c-gold">î</span> is simply tripled: A[1;0] = [3;0]. And [1; −1] is doubled: A[1;−1] = [2;−2]. These are the <b>eigenvectors</b> of A, and 3 and 2 are their <b>eigenvalues</b> — the stretch factors.
Canvas: Replay the morph; two gold g.lines (extent ±5) appear through [1,0] and [1,−1]; the fan arrows on those lines are drawn gold and slide along their lines without turning; labels "×3", "×2" at their tips.
Math panel: `A[1;0] = 3·[1;0]`, `A[1;−1] = 2·[1;−1]`.
### 3. Written down: Av = λv · 2.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: On an eigenvector the matrix acts like a plain number: <b>Av = λv</b>. The Greek letter λ (lambda) is the eigenvalue. The big machine A, on these special inputs, is just "multiply by 3" or "multiply by 2".
Canvas: The gold vector [1,−1] and its image [2,−2] with a g.dim "λ = 2"; the symbols appear next to the arrow then migrate to the panel.
Math panel: `Av = λv` with v in c-gold, λ as a plain number.
### 4. The line matters, not the length · 2.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Any vector on an eigenline is an eigenvector: [2; −2], [−0.5; 0.5], all of them get doubled and stay on the line. So we speak of eigen<i>lines</i> — directions — and pick any convenient length when we draw one.
Canvas: Five gold vectors of different lengths (including a negative multiple) on the [1,−1] line all stretch ×2 in place; the same on the î line.
Math panel: `A(cv) = c·Av = λ(cv)`.
### 5. Finding them: slide λ until A − λI collapses · 5 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Av = λv says (A − λI)v = 0: the matrix A − λI must squash v to zero, so its determinant must vanish. Slide λ. The grid of A − λI flattens exactly twice — at λ = 2 and λ = 3 — and the det bar touches zero there. The eigenvalues are where the picture collapses.
Canvas: s.lambda slider 0→4 sweeps automatically (t 0→1) and is live afterwards. Grid drawn under A − λI; a horizontal det bar (g.bars one value, gold when |det| < 0.05) reads (3−λ)(2−λ); at λ = 2 the grid flattens onto the line [1,−1] (gold) and at λ = 3 onto î (gold); a magenta null-space arrow appears at each collapse.
Math panel: `det(A − λI) = (3−λ)(2−λ)` live; `λ = 2, 3` gold when hit.
### 6. Misconception: every matrix has real eigenvectors · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: ✕ Rotate the plane by 90°. Every arrow turns; not one stays on its line. Slide λ from −3 to 3: det(R − λI) = λ² + 1 is never zero — the bar never touches the axis. Rotations have <i>no</i> real eigenvectors. Their eigenvalues are complex numbers, which we do not draw.
Canvas: s.mode 'rotation': fan rotates 90°, every ghost line crossed; a red g.marker.cross in the center labeled "no eigenline". λ sweeps −3→3; the det bar stays above zero (minimum 1 at λ = 0) drawn in red.
Math panel: `R = [[0,−1],[1,0]]`, `det(R − λI) = λ² + 1 > 0`.
### 7. Rotation-scalings spiral · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Mix a rotation with a stretch: A = 1.1·R(20°). Still no eigenline — but the complex eigenvalues have size 1.1, and that size shows: apply A again and again and every arrow spirals outward by 10 % per step. The size of λ controls growth even when λ is complex.
Canvas: s.mode 'spiral': one yellow vector hit 12 times, trail of ghosts forming an outward spiral (g.polyline through the tips); readout "|λ| = 1.1".
Math panel: `λ = 1.1·(cos20° ± i sin20°)`, `|λ| = 1.1`.
### 8. Symmetric matrices: perpendicular eigenvectors · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: For a <i>symmetric</i> matrix the eigenvectors are always perpendicular. A = [[2,1],[1,2]] stretches <span class="c-gold">[1;1]</span> by 3 and <span class="c-gold">[1;−1]</span> by 1 — a right angle apart — and turns the unit circle into an ellipse whose axes are exactly those two directions. Part 4 leans on this constantly.
Canvas: s.mode 'symmetric': circle → ellipse (g.ellipse(A)); gold axes at 45° and −45° with a right-angle marker; labels "λ = 3", "λ = 1".
Math panel: `A = Aᵀ ⇒ eigenvectors ⟂`.
### 9. In 3D a rotation has an axis — only in 3D · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Rotate a cube about a tilted line. Every point turns except those on the line: it is an eigenvector with λ = 1. This is special to three dimensions. In 2D a rotation fixes nothing but the origin; in four dimensions a rotation can turn two planes at once and fix no line at all. <b>Only in 3D does every rotation have an axis.</b>
Canvas: s.view '3d'; g.box under rot3(axis [1,1,0.5] normalized, θ 0→120°); the gold axis line stays fixed; a few points off the axis leave circular trails. Caption verbatim: "Only in 3D does every rotation have an axis."
Math panel: `R a = a (λ = 1)`.
### 10. Preview: repeating lines everything up · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Back to A = [[3,1],[0,2]]. Apply it six times to the whole fan. Everything swings toward the eigenline with the largest eigenvalue, because ×3 beats ×2 at every step. The next lesson makes that into a method — power iteration — and a reason Markov chains settle down.
Canvas: s.powerK 0→6: the fan is re-drawn as Aᵏ·fan normalized to unit length each step; arrows crowd onto the gold î line.
Math panel: `Aᵏv ≈ c·3ᵏ·[1;0]`.
### 11. Why ML cares · 3.5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Three places you will meet eigenvectors soon: the covariance matrix's eigenvectors are the principal components (p4-10); the Hessian's eigenvalues set the largest safe learning rate (p5-03); a weight matrix's eigenvalues decide whether repeated application explodes or vanishes (p5-06). Same gold lines, three meanings.
Canvas: Three thumbnails: a tilted cloud with gold axes; elliptical contours with gold axes and a red "2/λmax" tick on a slider; a unit circle growing/shrinking under Wᵏ.
Math panel: `Σ q = λ q`, `H`, `Wᵏ`.
### 12. Recap · 2.5 s · flags: recap
Narration: Eigenvectors keep their line; eigenvalues are the stretch. Find them where det(A − λI) = 0 — where the grid collapses. Rotations have none in 2D (complex λ), an axis in 3D only. Symmetric matrices: perpendicular eigenvectors.
Canvas: Three mini-cards: (1) a fan with two gold lines; (2) det bar touching zero at two λ's; (3) ellipse with perpendicular gold axes beside a rotation with a red ✕.
Math panel: `Av = λv`, `det(A − λI) = 0`.

## p4-03-diagonalization-powers — Diagonalization, matrix powers, power iteration
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p4-02-eigenvectors, p4-01-change-of-basis
**Goal.** The learner can write A = PDP⁻¹, compute Aᵏ by powering the diagonal, predict explode/vanish from |λ|, and run power iteration by hand: multiply, normalize, repeat, until the vector lands on the dominant eigenline.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: PageRank runs power iteration on a 10⁹×10⁹ stochastic matrix; a 100-step RNN applies Wᵏ with k = 100 · transfers: Aᵏ = PDᵏP⁻¹ and "the largest |λ| wins" hold in any dimension; only the number of eigenlines grows.
**Can't show.**
- Non-diagonalizable matrices beyond the 2D shear (we show the one-eigenline case and stop).
- Complex dominant eigenvalues (the fan spirals instead of converging) — mentioned, not animated beyond p4-02 step 7.
**Terms.** diagonalization — A = PDP⁻¹ with P's columns the eigenvectors and D diagonal with the eigenvalues; matrix power Aᵏ — apply A k times; dominant eigenvalue — the eigenvalue of largest size, which controls Aᵏ for large k; power iteration — repeat v ← Av/|Av| until it settles on the dominant eigenvector.
**State.** `s.A` (default [[2,1],[1,2]]), `s.P`, `s.D` (derived), `s.k` (0..12 power), `s.fan` (24 unit vectors), `s.normalize` (bool), `s.stage` (0..3 for the P⁻¹, D, P tweens), `s.t`, `s.v` (yellow start vector for power iteration), `s.history` (from LA.num.powerIter), `s.mode` ('grow'|'shrink'|'mixed'|'shear').
**Steps.**
### 1. Recall: in the eigenbasis A is just two numbers · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A = [[2,1],[1,2]] stretches <span class="c-gold">p₁</span> = [1;1] by 3 and <span class="c-gold">p₂</span> = [1;−1] by 1. In the gold basis the whole map is "×3 along p₁, ×1 along p₂": the diagonal matrix D = [[3,0],[0,1]]. From p4-01, A = PDP⁻¹ with P = [p₁ p₂].
Canvas: Gold grid (g.grid(P)) morphs under A: pure stretch along p₁; the gray standard grid shears beside it (ghost kept). Panel tiles P, D, P⁻¹.
Math panel: `A = P D P⁻¹`, `P = [[1,1],[1,−1]]`, `D = diag(3,1)`.
### 2. Three moves, read right to left · 4.5 s · flags: (none)

Narration: Turn the collapse into a method. Start anywhere, multiply by A, divide by the length so it never explodes, repeat. ## p4-04-simplex-softmax — Probability vectors, the simplex, softmax, stochastic matrices
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 3D · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p1-03-linear-combinations-span, p2-02-matrix-vector, p4-02-eigenvectors
**Goal.** The learner can place a 3-class probability vector as a point on the triangle, turn any three scores into such a point with softmax, predict what temperature does, and see that a column-stochastic matrix maps the triangle into itself — so a fixed point (eigenvalue 1) must exist.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 3D (3 classes → a triangle) · realSize: a language model's softmax has 50 000 entries; a 1000-class classifier lives on a 999-dimensional simplex · transfers: "non-negative, sums to 1" is the whole definition; softmax and temperature act entrywise the same way for any length.
**Can't show.**
- The simplex for 4 classes is a tetrahedron (drawable), for 5+ it is not; we stop at the triangle.
- Why softmax specifically (maximum entropy / log-linear reasons) — geometry only here.
**Terms.** probability vector — entries ≥ 0 that sum to 1; simplex — the set of all probability vectors: a triangle for 3 entries, a tetrahedron for 4; one-hot — a corner of the simplex: all probability on one class; softmax — exponentiate each score and divide by the sum, landing on the simplex; temperature T — divide scores by T before softmax: low T sharpens toward a corner, high T flattens toward the center; stochastic matrix — a square matrix whose columns are probability vectors; it maps the simplex into itself.

## p4-05-markov-pagerank — Markov chains converge to an eigenvector; PageRank ★
**Part** 4 · **Tags** capstone · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~13 · **Prereqs** p4-04-simplex-softmax, p4-03-diagonalization-powers, p2-02-matrix-vector
**Goal.** The learner can step a distribution with a stochastic matrix, watch it converge to the stationary distribution from any start, verify Pπ = π with λ = 1, read the speed from λ₂, and run PageRank on a 5-page graph as the same power iteration with bars.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 3D triangle (3 states) + bars (5 pages) · realSize: PageRank ran on ~10⁹ pages; a language model's next-token matrix is 50 000 × 50 000 · transfers: π_{t+1} = Pπ_t, Pπ = π and convergence at rate |λ₂| are the same for any number of states; only the simplex becomes undrawable.
**Can't show.**
- The 4-dimensional simplex of five pages (we show bars and say so).
- Chains that never settle (periodic) beyond one 2-cycle example; and why damping guarantees uniqueness (a theorem, stated not proved).
**Terms.** Markov chain — a system that jumps between states with fixed probabilities that depend only on the current state; transition matrix P — column j holds the probabilities of going from state j to each state; stationary distribution π — a distribution that P leaves unchanged, Pπ = π; PageRank — the stationary distribution of a random web surfer who follows links, with occasional random jumps (damping); random surfer — the imaginary walker whose long-run visit frequencies are PageRank.
**State.** `s.P` (3×3 column-stochastic; default weather matrix), `s.pi0` (start distribution), `s.k` (steps taken, 0..12), `s.trail` (list of distributions, derived), `s.pi` (stationary, derived via LA.num.stationary), `s.view`, `s.cam`, `s.mode` ('simplex'|'pagerank'), `s.links` (5×5 adjacency, boolean), `s.damping` (0.85), `s.rank` (5-vector), `s.rankHistory` (from LA.num.powerIter), `s.surferPos` (node index), `s.visits` (5 counters), `s.cycle` (bool for the periodic example).
**Steps.**
### 1. A weather machine · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Three kinds of day: sunny, cloudy, rainy. From a sunny day, tomorrow is sunny with 0.8, cloudy 0.1, rainy 0.1. Each state has its own column of chances: the <b>transition matrix</b> P. Columns sum to 1 — each is a point on the triangle from last lesson.

### 2. One day: the point jumps to a column · 3.5 s · flags: (none)

### 9. Damping and the ranking · 4.5 s · flags: (none)

## p4-06-symmetric-quadratic-forms — Symmetric matrices, quadratic forms, bowls and saddles
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p4-02-eigenvectors, p4-01-change-of-basis, p2-10-transpose-outer-symmetric
**Goal.** The learner can factor a symmetric matrix as QΛQᵀ (rotate, stretch, rotate back), read the shape of the surface z = xᵀAx from the signs of the eigenvalues (bowl / trough / saddle), and knows that positive entries do not make a matrix positive definite.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D ellipse + 3D surface · realSize: a loss Hessian for a 10⁶-parameter model is a 10⁶×10⁶ symmetric matrix; a covariance of 768 features is 768×768 · transfers: perpendicular eigenvectors, A = QΛQᵀ and "signs of λ decide bowl vs saddle" are exact in every dimension.
**Can't show.**

- Hyperbolic level sets in 3D (hyperboloids) — the 2D hyperbola stands in.
**Terms.** symmetric matrix — A = Aᵀ, mirror-equal across the diagonal; spectral decomposition — A = QΛQᵀ with Q orthonormal eigenvectors and Λ the diagonal of eigenvalues; quadratic form — the number xᵀAx attached to each point x, a surface over the plane; positive definite — xᵀAx > 0 for every x ≠ 0, all eigenvalues positive, a bowl; positive semi-definite — xᵀAx ≥ 0, eigenvalues ≥ 0, a bowl with a flat direction; indefinite — mixed signs, a saddle.

## p4-07-svd — SVD: rotate, stretch, rotate
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p4-06-symmetric-quadratic-forms, p3-04-orthonormal-gram-schmidt, p2-09-non-square
**Goal.** The learner can decompose any 2×2 (and see a 3×2) as rotate–stretch–rotate, name v₁, v₂, σ₁, σ₂, u₁, u₂ on the picture, distinguish singular values from eigenvalues, and read rank from the number of nonzero σ.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D (+ one 3D step) · realSize: a 784×128 weight matrix has 128 singular values; a 4096×4096 attention projection has 4096 · transfers: every matrix of any shape is UΣVᵀ; the circle→ellipse picture is exact for each pair (vᵢ, uᵢ).
**Can't show.**

- The ellipsoid image of a 4×4 or larger matrix.

**Terms.** singular value decomposition — A = UΣVᵀ: an orthogonal Vᵀ, a diagonal stretch Σ, an orthogonal U; singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ 0 — the stretch factors, lengths of the ellipse's semi-axes; right singular vectors v — the input directions that stay perpendicular after the map; left singular vectors u — where those directions land; rank — the number of nonzero singular values.
**State.** `s.A` (2×2, default [[2,1],[0,1.5]]), `s.svd` (derived via LA.num.svd2rot: {U, s, V, thetaV, thetaU, reflect}), `s.stage` (0..3 for Vᵀ, Σ, U), `s.t`, `s.sigma2Scale` (0..1 to flatten σ₂), `s.v` (yellow test vector), `s.view`, `s.cam`, `s.A32` (3×2 for the non-square step, default [[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]]), `s.compare` ('shear'|'rotation'|'symmetric').
**Steps.**
### 1. Every matrix makes an ellipse · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Any 2×2 matrix — rotation, shear, squash — sends the unit circle to an ellipse, possibly a flat one. A = [[2,1],[0,1.5]]: not symmetric, not a rotation, yet the circle becomes a tidy tilted ellipse with two perpendicular axes. Where did perpendicular axes come from?
Canvas: Circle → g.ellipse(A) over the grid morph (t 0→1); the ellipse's two semi-axes drawn gold (lengths σ₁ ≈ 2.38, σ₂ ≈ 1.26) with a right-angle marker; several gray radius vectors ride along to show general vectors turn.
Math panel: `A = [[2,1],[0,1.5]]`.
### 2. Three moves: rotate, stretch, rotate · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Here is the secret: any matrix can be done in three simple moves. First <i>rotate</i> the plane (Vᵀ) so two special input directions line up with the axes; then <i>stretch</i> along the axes (Σ) by σ₁ and σ₂; then <i>rotate</i> again (U) into the final position. No shear is ever needed.

### 3. The names on the picture · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The two special input directions are the <b>right singular vectors</b> <span class="c-gold">v₁</span>, <span class="c-gold">v₂</span>: perpendicular on the circle, still perpendicular after the map. The stretch factors σ₁ ≥ σ₂ are the <b>singular values</b>. Where v₁ and v₂ land, σ₁<span class="c-gold">u₁</span> and σ₂<span class="c-gold">u₂</span>, are the ellipse's axes. <b>A = UΣVᵀ</b>.

### 4. Not eigenvectors: pairs, always perpendicular, always exist · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Eigenvectors keep their <i>own</i> direction; singular vectors come in input/output pairs that may differ. ## p4-08-low-rank-compression — Low-rank approximation, SVD image compression, recommenders/LoRA ★
**Part** 4 · **Tags** capstone · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~13 · **Prereqs** p4-07-svd, p2-10-transpose-outer-symmetric, p2-08-rank-column-null
**Goal.** The learner can rebuild a 32×32 image from its first k rank-1 sheets, read the error and storage from the singular-value bars, explain a ratings table as dot products of user and movie points in a 2-D latent plane, and see a rank-1 update move a weight matrix along one direction only.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D heatmaps (the matrix is the picture — no projection) · realSize: a 1000×1000 image has 1000 σ's; LoRA adapts 4096×4096 weights with rank 8–64; a streaming service's ratings matrix is 10⁸ × 10⁵ · transfers: A = Σσᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ and "keep the biggest σ's" are exact at every size; only the number of sheets grows.
**Can't show.**
- Latent spaces with k > 2 (real recommenders use k ≈ 50–200); the 2-D latent plane is exact only for the toy.
- Why truncation is optimal (Eckart–Young) — stated as a theorem chip, not proved.
- Color images (three stacked matrices) — grayscale only.
**Terms.** rank-1 sheet — a matrix uvᵀ: one column times one row, every row a multiple of the same row; truncated SVD A_k — keep the k largest σ's and their u, v: the best rank-k approximation (Eckart–Young); Frobenius error — ‖A − A_k‖_F / ‖A‖_F, the square root of the summed squared pixel errors, relative; latent space — the hidden coordinate plane where users and items are points and a rating is their dot product; LoRA — fine-tuning a weight matrix W by adding a low-rank BA instead of changing all of W.
**State.** `s.img` (32×32 from LA.rng.smallImage(seed 48, {size:32})), `s.svd` (U, s, V via LA.num.svd), `s.k` (1..32), `s.on` (32 booleans, which sheets are included), `s.noise` (0..1), `s.err`, `s.storage`, `s.showSheet` (index of the sheet shown separately), `s.logScale`, `s.mode` ('image'|'recsys'|'lora'), `s.U6` (6×2 users), `s.V8` (8×2 movies), `s.mask` (hidden cells), `s.hotCell`, `s.W` (2×2 base), `s.alpha` (rank-1 nudge), `s.u`, `s.vv` (nudge directions), `s.subsample` (bool for the misconception).
**Steps.**
### 1. An image is a matrix · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: This 32×32 grayscale picture is 1024 numbers in a grid — a matrix <b>A</b>. Bright cells are big numbers, dark cells small. Everything we know about matrices applies to it, including the SVD. Let us take it apart.
Canvas: g.image(s.img) centered (world 8×8 units) with a label "32 × 32 = 1024 numbers"; a magnifier inset showing a 4×4 patch with its values printed.
Math panel: `A ∈ ℝ³²ˣ³²`.
### 2. Its singular values · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A = UΣVᵀ. The 32 singular values, as bars, fall off a cliff: the first few are large, most are tiny. Whatever is in this picture is mostly carried by a handful of directions. Toggle the log scale to see the tail.
Canvas: g.bars of σ₁…σ₃₂ (gold, descending) beneath the image, growing in (t 0→1); the first 4 bars labeled with values; log-scale toggle live.
Math panel: `σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ σ₃₂ ≥ 0`.
### 3. One sheet = a column times a row · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The first piece, σ₁<span class="c-gold">u₁v₁ᵀ</span>, is a 32-cell column times a 32-cell row: an outer product. Every row of it is the same row, scaled. On its own it is a blurry, stripy ghost of the picture — the "average structure" — and it costs 65 numbers instead of 1024.
Canvas: u₁ drawn as a vertical 32-cell strip (left) and v₁ᵀ as a horizontal strip (top); the sheet σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ fills cell by cell (fast, 1.5 s) as the row × column highlight sweeps; the result is shown as a heatmap labeled "sheet 1".
Math panel: `σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ`, `(32×1)(1×32) = 32×32`, `cost 32 + 32 + 1`.
### 4. Stack sheets: the picture sharpens · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Add sheet 2, then 3, then 4 … Each one adds detail the previous ones missed. Large structures appear by k = 3; the circle's edge needs about 8; fine texture waits for the small σ's. The reconstruction A_k = Σᵢ≤k σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ is the <b>truncated SVD</b>.
Canvas: Three panels: original | A_k | current sheet k. s.k 1→16 (0.3 s per step): A_k sharpens; the sheet panel shows σₖuₖvₖᵀ; the bars up to k turn gold, the rest gray; error readout falls.
Math panel: `A_k = Σ_{i≤k} σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ`, `k` live.
### 5. Error and storage · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two numbers to watch. The relative error ‖A − A_k‖_F / ‖A‖_F comes straight from the dropped bars: it is √(Σᵢ>k σᵢ²)/√(Σσᵢ²). The storage is k·(32+32+1): at k = 8 that is 520 numbers, about half, for a picture you can barely tell apart. No other rank-k matrix does better — that is the Eckart–Young theorem.
Canvas: Error bar (gray, shrinking as k grows) with the dropped σ bars highlighted; storage counter "k·65 = 520 of 1024"; a "Theorem" chip beside "best rank-k".
Math panel: `err_k = √(Σ_{i>k}σᵢ²)/√(Σσᵢ²)`, `storage = k(m+n+1)`.
### 6. Misconception: compression means dropping pixels · 3.5 s · flags: misconception
Narration: ✕ "To store half the numbers, keep half the pixels." Try it: a 16×16 subsample at the same 520-number budget is blocky and loses the thin diagonal entirely. The SVD keeps <i>every</i> pixel and drops <i>sheets</i>: what goes is the faint structure, not whole locations.
Canvas: Side by side at equal budget: s.subsample (every other pixel, blown back up, red g.marker.cross and error readout) vs A₈ (green check and its smaller error).
Math panel: `err(subsample) ≫ err(A₈)`.
### 7. Click a bar: sheets are independent · 3.5 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Click any bar to switch that sheet off or on. Remove sheet 1 and the average brightness goes with it, leaving only the detail. Remove sheet 5 alone and one stripe pattern vanishes. The sheets add independently — each is perpendicular to the others, in both its row and its column.
Canvas: Bars are clickable (g.handle per bar, 'view' plane); s.on toggles; A_on recomputed per frame; the sheet panel shows the last toggled sheet.
Math panel: `A_on = Σ_{i∈on} σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ`.
### 8. Noise lives in the small σ's · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Sprinkle random noise on the picture. The big singular values barely move; the tail lifts. Keeping k = 8 now <i>removes</i> most of the noise, because random speckle has no large sheets. Truncation denoises for the same reason it compresses: structure is low-rank, noise is not.
Canvas: s.noise 0→0.3: noisy image on the left; bars: the first 5 nearly unchanged, the tail rises (highlighted); A₈ on the right visibly cleaner than the noisy original.
Math panel: `A + N`, `σ tail ↑`.
### 9. A ratings table is low-rank · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Six people rated eight films, with gaps. Place each person and each film as a point in a 2-D plane of taste (action↔drama, light↔dark). A rating is 3 + the dot product of the two points — the shadow of one on the other. All 48 cells come from 28 coordinates: the table has rank 2, and the gaps are filled by the same dot products.
Canvas: s.mode 'recsys': a 6×8 heatmap (sequential palette 1–5, 14 masked cells gray) beside a 2-D latent plane with 6 user points (purple) and 8 film points (yellow) at the authored coordinates (U6, V8 below). Math panel: `R ≈ 3 + U Vᵀ`, `(6×2)(2×8)`, `r_ij = 3 + uᵢ·vⱼ`.
### 10. LoRA: a rank-1 nudge to a weight matrix · 4 s · flags: interactive
Narration: A weight matrix W is a grid transformation. Fine-tuning changes it — but a rank-1 change α·<span class="c-gold">u</span><span class="c-gold">vᵀ</span> moves the grid along <i>one</i> output direction u, by an amount read off one input direction v. Slide α: the grid shifts only along u. Few numbers, targeted change.
Canvas: s.mode 'lora': grid under W = [[1,0.2],[0,1]] (ghost) morphs to W + α·uvᵀ with u = [0.8,0.6], v = [0.6,0.8] as α slides −1→1; gold u and v drawn; points along v's perpendicular do not move (dashed highlight).
Math panel: `W' = W + α u vᵀ`, `rank(uvᵀ) = 1`.
### 11. Why ML cares · 3.5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Trained weight matrices have decaying singular values, just like the picture, so a rank-8 update can steer a 4096×4096 layer with 65 536 numbers instead of 16 million — that is LoRA. Recommenders factor a huge sparse table into two thin ones. The same bars, the same sheets, bigger.
Canvas: Thumbnails: a 4096-bar spectrum sketch decaying; tiles W (4096×4096) + B (4096×8)·A (8×4096) with counts; a tall thin U and short wide Vᵀ around a big R.
Math panel: `W + BA`, `rank ≤ r`.
### 12. Recap · 2.5 s · flags: recap
Narration: A matrix is a stack of rank-1 sheets weighted by σ. Keep the largest few: best rank-k approximation, small error, small storage, less noise. Ratings tables and weight updates are low-rank: a few directions explain the whole.
Canvas: Three mini-cards: (1) column × row → sheet; (2) bars with the first k gold and a sharpening image; (3) user and film points with a shadow.
Math panel: `A_k = Σ_{i≤k} σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ`.

## p4-09-covariance — Mean, centering, covariance as a shape; Gaussians
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p3-07-gram-matrix, p4-06-symmetric-quadratic-forms, p1-07-data-as-vectors
**Goal.** The learner can center a cloud, measure the three covariance numbers from it, draw the covariance ellipse with its gold eigen-axes, and generate the same cloud from round noise via x = μ + Lz.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D cloud + one 3D ellipsoid · realSize: a covariance of 768 embedding dimensions is 768×768; a Gaussian over 784 pixels has a 784×784 Σ · transfers: Σ = XcᵀXc/n, symmetric PSD, eigenvectors = axes, x = μ + Lz — all identical in any dimension.
**Can't show.**
- The ellipsoid for d > 3 (we show a 64×64 Σ as a heatmap).
- Heavy tails / non-Gaussian clouds: the ellipse only summarizes second moments, whatever the shape.
**Terms.** mean μ — the average point, the cloud's center; centering — subtracting μ from every point so the cloud sits at the origin; variance — the average squared spread of one feature; covariance — the average product of two features' deviations: positive when they rise together, negative when opposite; covariance matrix Σ — variances on the diagonal, covariances off it; Σ = XcᵀXc/n; Gaussian — the bell-shaped cloud whose 1σ contour is the ellipse of Σ; Cholesky factor L — a "square root" of Σ with LLᵀ = Σ, used to generate samples μ + Lz.
**State.** `s.pts` (80 points from LA.rng.gaussianCloud(seed 49, n 80, {mean:[2,1], cov:[[2,1.2],[1.2,1]]})), `s.mu` (derived), `s.centerT` (0..1 tween), `s.Sigma` (derived via LA.num.cov), `s.eig` (LA.num.eigSym, stabilized), `s.L` (LA.num.chol), `s.z` (80 unit-noise points, seed 50), `s.sampleT` (0..2: z → Lz → μ + Lz), `s.rings` (1σ, 2σ booleans), `s.sx`, `s.sy`, `s.rho` (playground generator), `s.standardize` (bool), `s.view`, `s.cam`, `s.Sigma3`, `s.hotAxis` ('x'|'y'|'xy'|null).
**Steps.**
### 1. A cloud and its center · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Eighty data points with two features, drawn as a tilted cloud. First question: where is its center? Average all the x's and all the y's: the <b>mean</b> μ ≈ [2, 1]. One point that stands for the whole cloud.
Canvas: Points appear one by one (t 0→0.6); a white g.vector from the origin to μ grows (t 0.6→1) with label μ.
Math panel: `μ = (1/n) Σᵢ xᵢ = [2.0; 1.0]` (live values from the seed).
### 2. Centering: slide the cloud to the origin · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Subtract μ from every point. The cloud slides so its center sits on the origin; its shape is untouched. Centered data makes every later formula simpler: from here on, "spread" means "distance from the origin".
Canvas: s.centerT 0→1: all points translate by −μ·t, a dashed ghost of the original cloud stays; μ's arrow shrinks to nothing.
Math panel: `X_c = X − μ`.
### 3. Variance along each axis · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: How wide is the cloud left-to-right? Square each x-deviation and average: the <b>variance</b> of feature 1, about 2. Up-and-down: the variance of feature 2, about 1. Two numbers for two axes — but they miss the tilt.
Canvas: s.hotAxis 'x': each point drops a dashed line to the x-axis; squares of side |x| flash and shrink into a bar labeled "var(x) ≈ 2.0" on the x-axis; then the same for y with "var(y) ≈ 1.0".
Math panel: `Σ₁₁ = (1/n)Σ xᵢ² ≈ 2.0`, `Σ₂₂ = (1/n)Σ yᵢ² ≈ 1.0`.
### 4. Covariance: do they move together? · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Multiply each point's x by its y and average. Points in the top-right and bottom-left give positive products, the other quadrants negative. The cloud leans up-right, so the average is positive: <b>covariance</b> ≈ 1.2. Put all three numbers in a symmetric matrix: <b>Σ</b> = XcᵀXc/n — the Gram matrix of the two feature columns.
Canvas: Quadrants tint faintly orange (xy > 0) and blue (xy < 0); each point's rectangle |x|·|y| flashes in its sign color; a running-average bar settles at 1.2. The 2×2 Σ fills: diagonal from step 3, off-diagonals both 1.2 (mirror flash).
Math panel: `Σ₁₂ = Σ₂₁ = (1/n)Σ xᵢyᵢ ≈ 1.2`, `Σ = [[2.0,1.2],[1.2,1.0]] = XcᵀXc/n`.
### 5. The ellipse of Σ · 4.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Σ is symmetric, so p4-06 applies: it has perpendicular gold eigenvectors and it turns the unit circle into an ellipse. Draw that ellipse (scaled by the square roots of the eigenvalues): it hugs the cloud. Its long axis is the direction of greatest spread; the axis lengths are the spreads √λ₁ ≈ 1.68 and √λ₂ ≈ 0.44.
Canvas: Unit circle → g.ellipse(Σ^½) (computed as Q·diag(√λ)·Qᵀ) in teal outline; gold axes with lengths labeled; the ellipse encloses ≈ 39 % of points (1σ) — label "1σ".
Math panel: `Σ = QΛQᵀ`, `axes = √λᵢ qᵢ`, `λ ≈ 2.83, 0.17`.
### 6. Misconception: scaling each feature removes the tilt · 3.5 s · flags: misconception
Narration: ✕ "Divide x by its spread and y by its spread, and the features are independent." Do it: the ellipse changes shape but stays tilted. Standardizing fixes the diagonal to 1; the off-diagonal becomes the correlation 0.85 and stays. Un-tilting needs a rotation — whitening, in p4-11.
Canvas: s.standardize: points scaled per axis; ellipse re-drawn (still tilted, now with diagonal entries 1); Σ shows [[1,0.85],[0.85,1]]; red g.marker.cross on the off-diagonal cell with label "still correlated".
Math panel: `Σ_std = [[1, ρ],[ρ, 1]]`, `ρ = 0.85`.
### 7. Making the cloud: x = μ + Lz · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Run it backwards. Start from round noise z — 80 points with Σ = I. Multiply by <b>L</b>, a square root of Σ with LLᵀ = Σ (here L = [[1.41,0],[0.85,0.53]]): the round cloud becomes the tilted ellipse cloud. Add μ: it slides into place. Every Gaussian is a stretched, rotated, shifted ball of unit noise.
Canvas: s.sampleT 0→2: round cloud (gray) with its unit circle → each point tweened by lerp(I, L) (circle → ellipse) → translated by μ; the result overlays the original cloud's ghost (same shape); 1σ and 2σ rings drawn.
Math panel: `z ~ N(0, I)`, `x = μ + Lz`, `LLᵀ = Σ`.
### 8. det Σ: how much room the cloud takes · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The 1σ ellipse has area π·√det Σ. If the features were perfectly dependent the cloud would lie on a line, Σ would have det 0, and the ellipse would be flat — no area at all. That determinant normalizes the Gaussian's bell curve, and det 0 is why duplicated features break Gaussian models.
Canvas: Ellipse area shaded gold with label "area = π√0.56 ≈ 2.35"; then the playground ρ sweeps to 1: the ellipse flattens to a gold segment, det → 0, red tint.
Math panel: `det Σ = 2.0·1.0 − 1.2² = 0.56`, `area = π√det Σ`.
### 9. In 3D: an ellipsoid with three axes · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Three features give a 3×3 Σ and an ellipsoid with three perpendicular gold axes. This cloud is long along one axis, medium along another, and very thin along the third — it is nearly a pancake. PCA, next, will decide which axes to keep.
Canvas: s.view '3d': 150 points from μ + Lz with L = R·diag(3,1.2,0.3), R = rot3([0,0,1],35°)·rot3([1,0,0],25°), seed 50; g.ellipsoid(Σ3^½) with rings and meridians; gold axes with √λ labels. Orbit.
Math panel: `Σ₃ = QΛQᵀ`, `√λ ≈ 3, 1.2, 0.3`.
### 10. 64 features: a heatmap, not a shape · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: For 64 pixel features Σ is a 64×64 heatmap: bright diagonal (variances), structured off-diagonals (pixels that light up together). The picture stops at 3. The formula does not — Σ = XcᵀXc/n with more terms, and its eigenvectors are still the directions of spread.
Canvas: g.heat2d of the 64×64 covariance of LA.rng.syntheticDigits(seed 49, n 90, {size 8}), diverging palette; caption verbatim "The picture stops at 3. The formula does not."
Math panel: `Σ ∈ ℝ⁶⁴ˣ⁶⁴`.
### 11. Why ML cares · 3.5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: PCA reads Σ's axes to compress data (next lesson). Mahalanobis distance and whitening use Σ to measure distance in the cloud's own units (p4-11). Variational autoencoders sample with exactly x = μ + Lz so gradients can flow through randomness. And feature scaling is just taming Σ's diagonal.
Canvas: Three thumbnails: cloud flattened onto a gold plane; two probes with an ellipse; a round noise ball → ellipse labeled "reparameterization".
Math panel: `x = μ + Lz`.
### 12. Recap · 2.5 s · flags: recap
Narration: Center at μ. Σ: variances on the diagonal, covariance off it, Σ = XcᵀXc/n. Its gold eigen-axes and √λ draw the ellipse that hugs the cloud; det Σ is its area. Any Gaussian is unit noise stretched by L and shifted by μ.
Canvas: Three mini-cards: (1) cloud sliding to the origin; (2) ellipse with gold axes over the cloud and the 2×2 Σ; (3) round ball → tilted ellipse with "μ + Lz".

**Playground.** controls: slider · bind `sx` · 0.2..3 · "spread x"; slider · bind `sy` · 0.2..3 · "spread y"; slider · bind `rho` · −0.99..0.99 · "correlation ρ"; handle · bind `pts[i]` · drag any point · "points"; toggle · bind `rings` · "1σ / 2σ rings"; toggle · bind `view` · "3D ellipsoid". ## p4-10-pca — PCA finds the long axis and flattens a 3D cloud ★
**Part** 4 · **Tags** capstone · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~14 · **Prereqs** p4-09-covariance, p4-06-symmetric-quadratic-forms, p4-01-change-of-basis, p4-07-svd
**Goal.** The learner can find the first principal component as the line of greatest spread, rotate a 3D cloud into its gold axes, flatten it onto a plane while seeing exactly what is lost (ghost points, variance bars), and read a 2D scatter of 64-dimensional digits as an honest shadow.

## p4-11-whitening-mahalanobis — Whitening, Mahalanobis distance, anomaly detection ★
**Part** 4 · **Tags** capstone · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p4-09-covariance, p4-10-pca, p1-05-norms-distance
**Goal.** The learner can explain why two points at equal Euclidean distance from the center can be very differently "unusual", compute Mahalanobis distance as Euclidean distance after whitening, perform whitening as rotate → rescale (→ rotate back), and flag anomalies with a threshold.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: anomaly detection on 50 sensor channels uses a 50×50 Σ; LDA and Gaussian classifiers do this in d = 10³ · transfers: d_M² = (x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ) and W = Σ^{−1/2} are identical in any dimension; the ellipse becomes an ellipsoid we cannot draw.
**Can't show.**
- Density surfaces in d > 2 (the 3D bell is implied by the contour rings).
- Why the χ² rule picks a threshold (statistics, not geometry).
**Terms.** Mahalanobis distance — distance from the center measured in units of the cloud's spread in that direction: d_M² = (x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ); whitening — a linear map W with WΣWᵀ = I that turns the cloud into a round unit ball; Σ^{−1/2} — the symmetric whitening matrix QΛ^{−1/2}Qᵀ: rotate, divide each axis by √λ, rotate back; anomaly — a point whose Mahalanobis distance exceeds a threshold; standardization — dividing each feature by its own spread (diagonal only, leaves the tilt).
**State.** `s.pts` (200 points from LA.rng.gaussianCloud(seed 51, n 200, {mean:[0,0], cov:[[1,0.85],[0.85,1]]})), `s.Sigma`, `s.eig` (λ ≈ 1.85, 0.15; q₁ = [1;1]/√2), `s.W` (Σ^{−1/2}), `s.p1`, `s.p2` (probes: default 1.2·q₁ = [0.85,0.85] and 1.2·q₂ = [0.85,−0.85]), `s.whitenT` (0..3: rotate, scale, rotate back), `s.rings` (1,2,3σ), `s.threshold` (default 2.5), `s.flagged` (derived), `s.showDensity` (bool), `s.mode` ('euclid'|'mahal').
**Steps.**
### 1. Two probes, same distance · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A tilted cloud of 200 points and two new observations, <span class="c-v">P₁</span> and <span class="c-w">P₂</span>. Both are exactly 1.2 units from the center. Yet one sits comfortably among the data and the other hangs in empty space. Plain distance cannot tell them apart.
Canvas: Cloud (gray-white); teal 1σ ellipse; yellow point P₁ at [0.85,0.85] along the long axis, purple point P₂ at [0.85,−0.85] across it; a dashed circle of radius 1.2 through both; g.dim labels "1.2" on both radii.
Math panel: `|P₁ − μ| = |P₂ − μ| = 1.2`.
### 2. Rings of equal "unusualness" · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The cloud's own rulers are the ellipses of Σ: the 1σ, 2σ, 3σ rings. Points on the same ring are equally typical. P₁ lies inside the 1σ ring; P₂ is beyond 3σ. The right distance counts rings, not units.
Canvas: 1σ, 2σ, 3σ ellipses (teal, decreasing alpha) drawn from Σ^½; P₁ labeled "0.9σ", P₂ labeled "3.1σ"; a red tint on P₂.
Math panel: `rings: (x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ) = 1, 4, 9`.
### 3. The Mahalanobis distance · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Measure distance along each gold axis in units of that axis's spread: along <span class="c-gold">q₁</span> divide by √λ₁ = 1.36, along <span class="c-gold">q₂</span> by √λ₂ = 0.39. Then use Pythagoras. That is the <b>Mahalanobis distance</b>, d_M² = (x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ). P₁: 1.2/1.36 = 0.88. P₂: 1.2/0.39 = 3.1.
Canvas: Gold axes with their √λ unit ticks; each probe's coordinates in the gold frame shown as dashed drops with the division animated (length ÷ √λ → a number); bars for d_M of each probe.
Math panel: `d_M² = Σᵢ ((x−μ)·qᵢ)² / λᵢ`, `d_M(P₁) = 0.88`, `d_M(P₂) = 3.1`.
### 4. Whitening, move 1: rotate into the gold frame · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Here is the same idea as a transformation of the whole picture. First rotate everything by Qᵀ so the gold axes become the grid axes. The cloud is now axis-aligned, still elongated; the probes ride along.
Canvas: s.whitenT 0→1: cloud, rings, probes rotate by −45°; the ellipse's long axis lies on the x-axis.
Math panel: `x' = Qᵀ(x − μ)`.
### 5. Move 2: divide each axis by its spread · 3.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Now squeeze the long axis by 1/√λ₁ and stretch the short one by 1/√λ₂. The ellipses become circles of radius 1, 2, 3 and the cloud becomes a round ball. In this picture ordinary Euclidean distance <i>is</i> Mahalanobis distance: P₁ now sits at 0.88 from the center, P₂ at 3.1.
Canvas: s.whitenT 1→2: axis-aligned scale diag(1/√λ₁, 1/√λ₂) applied to everything; rings turn into unit circles; probes move; Euclidean readouts match the Mahalanobis numbers from step 3 (gold rings on equality).
Math panel: `x'' = Λ^{−1/2} x'`, `|x''| = d_M(x)`.
### 6. Move 3 (optional): rotate back — Σ^{−1/2} · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Rotating back by Q keeps the cloud round and returns the original orientation of the features. The whole map is <b>W = QΛ^{−1/2}Qᵀ = Σ^{−1/2}</b>: a symmetric matrix that whitens — WΣWᵀ = I. Any W with that property whitens; this one is the "least rotating" choice.
Canvas: s.whitenT 2→3: rotate by +45°; the round cloud stays round; the W matrix tile fills; a check "WΣWᵀ = I" computed live.
Math panel: `W = QΛ^{−1/2}Qᵀ`, `WΣWᵀ = I`.
### 7. Misconception: standardizing is whitening · 3.5 s · flags: misconception
Narration: ✕ "Divide each feature by its own standard deviation and you are done." Both features here already have spread 1, so standardizing changes <i>nothing</i>: the cloud stays tilted and P₂ still looks close. Whitening needs the rotation — it uses the off-diagonal of Σ, which standardizing ignores.
Canvas: Reset to the original cloud; "standardize" applies diag(1/σₓ, 1/σᵧ) = I: nothing moves; a red g.marker.cross with label "still tilted, P₂ still 'near'"; the Σ tile highlights the off-diagonal 0.85 in red.

### 8. Anomaly detection: a threshold on d_M · 4 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Set a threshold: anything beyond 2.5σ is an anomaly. Drag the threshold and watch points flip red as the ring shrinks; drag P₂ along the circle of radius 1.2 and see it turn from normal to anomalous as it crosses from the long axis to the short one. Distance in the data's own units.
Canvas: Threshold ring (gold dashed ellipse at d_M = s.threshold); points with d_M > threshold turn red (g.points colors); counter "flagged: n"; P₂ draggable (constrained to radius 1.2 in the original picture) with its live d_M.
Math panel: `anomaly ⇔ d_M(x) > τ`, `τ = 2.5` live.
### 9. The density as a bell · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The Gaussian's probability density depends on x only through d_M: it falls like e^{−d_M²/2}, and the rings are its level curves. The determinant det Σ from p4-09 scales its height so the total is 1. Low density = large d_M = anomaly: three names, one picture.
Canvas: g.contours of the density at 5 levels (match the rings) with a g.heat2d underlay; optional s.showDensity 3D toggle draws g.surface of the bell (res 24).
Math panel: `p(x) ∝ exp(−d_M²/2) / √det Σ`.
### 10. Why ML cares · 3.5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Fraud and fault detection flag large Mahalanobis distances. Linear discriminant analysis classifies by it. Whitening decorrelates inputs so gradient descent sees a round bowl instead of a valley (p5-03), and some normalization layers approximate it. The 2D picture is the exact mechanism in d = 50.
Canvas: Thumbnails: sensor channels as a 50-cell strip with one red flagged reading; two class ellipses with a boundary; elliptical loss contours turning round.
Math panel: `d_M² = (x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ)`.
### 11. Recap · 2.5 s · flags: recap
Narration: Equal Euclidean distance is not equal rarity. Mahalanobis divides by the spread along each gold axis — Euclidean distance after whitening. Whitening: rotate, rescale by 1/√λ, (rotate back); W = Σ^{−1/2}. Standardizing alone leaves the tilt. Threshold d_M to flag anomalies.
Canvas: Three mini-cards: (1) two probes on one circle with rings; (2) ellipse → circle under W; (3) red flagged points outside a gold ring.
Math panel: `W = Σ^{−1/2}`.
**Playground.** controls: slider · bind `rho` · −0.95..0.95 · "correlation"; slider · bind `whitenT` · 0..3 · "whitening (rotate / scale / back)"; handle · bind `p1` · drag · "P₁"; handle · bind `p2` · drag · "P₂"; slider · bind `threshold` · 1..4 · "threshold τ"; toggle · bind `showDensity` · "density surface". Presets: "ρ = 0.85", "Round (ρ = 0)", "Nearly flat (ρ = 0.98)", "Whitened". Live: rings, both distances per probe, flagged count, W tile, WΣWᵀ check.
**Checks.**

## p4-12-pseudoinverse-conditioning — Condition number, pseudoinverse via SVD, ridge
**Part** 4 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p4-07-svd, p3-06-regression-matrix-form, p3-01-systems-row-column
**Goal.** The learner can read the condition number as the aspect ratio of the ellipse, see why a thin ellipse turns small input noise into huge errors in the inverse, build the pseudo-inverse by reversing the three SVD moves (skipping σ = 0), and watch ridge regression tame 1/σ into σ/(σ² + λ).
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: a 10⁴-column design matrix with raw features can have κ ≈ 10⁶–10⁸; float32 holds about 7 digits · transfers: κ = σ_max/σ_min, A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ and the ridge filter σ/(σ²+λ) are identical for any shape; only the number of σ's grows.
**Can't show.**
- More than two singular directions as arrows (κ of a 128-column matrix is shown as a bar strip).
- Rounding error itself — we show its amplification, not its origin.
**Terms.** condition number κ — σ_max/σ_min: how much relative error in the input can grow in the output; ill-conditioned — large κ: tiny changes in b cause large changes in x; pseudo-inverse A⁺ — reverse the three SVD moves, inverting each σ > 0 and skipping σ = 0; A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ; ridge regression — replace 1/σ by σ/(σ²+λ), equivalently solve (XᵀX + λI)w = Xᵀy: shrink the weights, especially along weak directions; standardization — rescale features to unit spread so κ shrinks.
**State.** `s.A` (default [[1,1],[1,1.1]]), `s.svd` (LA.num.svd2rot), `s.kappa` (LA.num.condNumber), `s.b` ([2;2.1]), `s.x` (derived solve), `s.nudge` (0..1 tween of a 1 % change in b₂), `s.noiseR` (0.1), `s.noisePts` (24 points on the noise disk), `s.lambda` (ridge, default 0), `s.showInverseEllipse`, `s.stage` (0..3 reverse moves), `s.sigma2Scale`, `s.scaleA` (×1000 for the misconception), `s.featScale` (1..100 for the data step), `s.dir` (draggable perturbation direction).
**Steps.**
### 1. Two nearly parallel lines · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Back to the row picture: x + y = 2 and x + 1.1y = 2.1 cross at [1; 1]. The lines are almost parallel. Change the second right-hand side by 1 % — 2.1 to 2.12 — and the crossing leaps to [0.79; 1.21]: a 30 % jump in the answer from a 1 % change in the data.

### 3. Noise in b becomes a long error in x · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Suppose <span class="c-b">b</span> is only known to within a small disk of radius 0.1. ### 4. Inverting is reversing the three moves · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A = UΣVᵀ. To undo it, undo each move in reverse order: Uᵀ (a rotation back), then stretch by 1/σ₁ and 1/σ₂, then V. The dangerous step is the middle one: 1/σ₂ = 20 is where the sliver comes from. Bars of 1/σ show it at a glance.

Math panel: `A⁻¹ = V Σ⁻¹ Uᵀ`, `Σ⁻¹ = diag(1/σ₁, 1/σ₂)`.
### 5. σ = 0: skip it — the pseudo-inverse · 4 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Push σ₂ all the way to 0. The ellipse is flat; nothing can be undone along <span class="c-gold">u₂</span>. The <b>pseudo-inverse</b> simply refuses: Σ⁺ puts 0 where σ was 0, inverts the rest, and A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ. For a tall X this is (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ, for a wide X it is Xᵀ(XXᵀ)⁻¹ — p3-06's two formulas are one.

Math panel: `Σ⁺ = diag(1/σ₁, 0)`, `A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ`.
### 6. Misconception: det ≠ 0 means the answer is reliable · 3.5 s · flags: misconception
Narration: ✕ "The determinant is 0.1, not zero, so the system is fine." Multiply A by 1000: det becomes 100 000 — yet the ellipse has the same shape and κ is still 42; the noise sliver is unchanged. The determinant measures area; κ measures shape. Only κ predicts reliability.

Math panel: `det(1000A) = 10⁵`, `κ(1000A) = κ(A) = 42`.
### 7. Ridge: tame 1/σ into σ/(σ² + λ) · 5 s · flags: interactive
Narration: <b>Ridge regression</b> changes the dangerous middle move. Instead of 1/σ use σ/(σ² + λ). For big σ that is almost 1/σ; for tiny σ it is almost σ/λ — small, not huge. At λ = 0.1 the 1/σ₂ bar drops from 20.5 to 0.48, and the sliver of answers shrinks to a modest blob. Slide λ.

Math panel: `A⁺_λ = V diag(σᵢ/(σᵢ²+λ)) Uᵀ`, `= (AᵀA + λI)⁻¹Aᵀ`.
### 8. What ridge does to the answer · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The price: the ridge answer is pulled toward the origin, mostly along <span class="c-gold">v₂</span>, the direction the data barely determines. In weight space the solution slides from the exact x toward 0 as λ grows. That trade — a little bias for a lot less variance — is p5-04's regularization, seen from the SVD side.

Math panel: `x_λ → 0 as λ → ∞`.
### 9. Where κ comes from: feature scales · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: In a design matrix, κ is often just units. Measure one feature in grams and the other in kilograms and X's ellipse is 1000× thinner than it needs to be. <b>Standardize</b> — divide each column by its spread — and the ellipse rounds out, κ falls toward 1. For 128 features κ is σ₁/σ₁₂₈ on a bar strip. The picture stops at 3. The formula does not.

Narration: Three habits explained by one ratio: standardize features (κ of X), add ridge or weight decay (lift small σ), and watch the learning rate — gradient descent zigzags on a bowl whose Hessian has large κ, the next part's capstone. And float32 keeps ~7 digits, so κ ≈ 10⁷ leaves none.
