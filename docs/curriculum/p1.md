# Part 1 — Vectors (9 lessons)

Part 1 builds the learner's first object, the vector, and ends with their first model. Through-line: an arrow is a point is a list of numbers (p1-01); arrows add and scale (p1-02); recipes of arrows sweep out flat regions — spans, subspaces (p1-03); independent arrows make a basis and count dimensions (p1-04); arrows have lengths and distances in several flavors (p1-05); the dot product measures alignment and is the atom of every neuron, similarity and attention score (p1-06); data rows are vectors in ℝⁿ, honestly (p1-07); what we cannot draw in high dimensions we measure live (p1-08); a neuron is a weight vector, a dot product and a boundary (p1-09). Recommended order is the id order; p1-08 may be skimmed on a first pass but p1-09 depends on p1-06 and p1-07.

Shared data (seeded, generated inside each lesson with `LA.rng`):
- `knn30` (p1-05): `LA.rng(5)`; class A 15 points gaussian mean [1.5, 1.5] sd 0.7; class B 15 points mean [−1, −0.5] sd 0.7; probe at [0.4, 0.6].
- `flowers60` (p1-07): `LA.rng(7)`; 4 features f₁ length, f₂ width, f₃ height, f₄ weight; class A 30 points mean [2, 1, 3, 0.5] sd 0.4; class B 30 points mean [3.5, 2.5, 1.5, 1.5] sd 0.5. Blue = A, rose = B.
- `twoBlobs40` (p1-09): `LA.rng(9)`; class A 20 points mean [−1.5, 1] sd 0.6; class B 20 points mean [1.5, −1] sd 0.6.
- `digit7` (p1-07, p1-08): `LA.rng.syntheticDigits(seed 2)`, 28×28 stroke template "7" + noise, labeled "synthetic".

## p1-01-what-is-a-vector — Arrow, point, list of numbers
**Part** 1 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~8 · **Prereqs** p0-02-reading-the-symbols
**Goal.** The learner switches freely between the three faces of a vector — arrow from the origin, point, column of numbers — in 2D and 3D, and knows a vector has no position.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: a feature vector has 10–1000 entries; a word embedding 300–4096; a weight vector of one neuron 784 · transfers: "one entry per axis" and "arrow = point = list" hold in any n; only the arrow drawing stops at 3.
**Can't show.**
- an arrow with 784 components (we show a strip of numbers instead)
**Terms.** vector — one object with three faces: an arrow from the origin, a point, and a column of numbers; component — one entry of a vector, how far it goes along one axis; origin — the point where all axes meet, written 0; basis vector — one of the axis arrows of length 1: î, ĵ, k̂.
**State.** `v` (2- or 3-vector, default [3, 2] / [2, 1, 1.5]) · `view` · `cam` · `face` ('arrow'|'point'|'list'|'all') · `showComponents` · `ghosts` (array of translation offsets for the no-position step) · `strip` (5 values for the n-D step) · `probe` (entry-check vector)

**Steps.**
### 1. A point, then an arrow · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A yellow point appears at 3 along the green axis and 2 along the red. Now draw an arrow from the origin to it. Point and arrow are the same information: where to go, starting from the origin. We will draw whichever face helps most.
Canvas: 2D range −4..4. The point fades in at (3, 2) (t<0.3); the yellow arrow grows from the origin to the point (`g.vector`, t∈[0.3,0.9]); label "v" at the tip.
Math panel: —

### 2. Components become numbers · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Drop dashed lines to the axes. The green piece is 3, the red piece is 2. These are the <b>components</b> of <span class="c-v">v</span>: how far along î and how far along ĵ. Stack them in a bracket, green on top: that is the list face of the same vector.
Canvas: `g.vector` with `components:'xy'`: dashed green from origin to (3,0) and dashed red from (3,0) to (3,2) with `g.dim` labels "3" and "2" (t<0.5); the labels fly into the bracket (`ctx.fx.fly`, t∈[0.5,0.9]).
Math panel: `v = [3; 2]` tall bracket with colored entries.

### 3. Three faces, one object · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Watch the same vector show its three faces: only the arrow, only the point, only the list. Nothing changes but the drawing. When we talk about data we think "point"; when we add or compare we think "arrow"; when we compute we think "list".
Canvas: `face` cycles arrow → point → list → all, 1 s each: the arrow fades out leaving the point; the point fades out leaving the bracket drawn on canvas beside the ghost position; then all three return.
Math panel: `v = [3; 2]` with the caption "arrow · point · list" and the current face highlighted.

### 4. Negative components go the other way · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Drag the tip left of the origin and the green component becomes negative: −2 means two steps against î. Negative is a direction, not a mistake. Here <span class="c-v">v</span> = [−2; 1.5] points up and to the left.
Canvas: the tip tweens from (3, 2) to (−2, 1.5); the green dashed component flips to point left with label "−2"; the bracket updates live.
Math panel: `v = [−2; 1.5]`

### 5. A vector has no position · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief to test: "an arrow drawn somewhere else is a different vector." Copy the arrow and slide the copies around the plane. Same length, same direction — same components — so they are the same vector. We draw vectors from the origin by convention, nothing more.
Canvas: four ghost copies of the arrow (dim yellow, `ghost:true`) slide out from the origin to offsets (3,−2), (−3,1), (1,3), (−2,−3) along straight paths (t∈[0,0.6]); each shows the same dashed components; a label "different vectors?" gets a red ✕ at t=0.8 while all five brackets read [−2; 1.5].
Math panel: five identical brackets `[−2; 1.5]` in a row.

### 6. A third component · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: In 3D a vector has a third, blue component along k̂. <span class="c-v">v</span> = [2; 1; 1.5] goes 2 along the green axis, 1 along the red, 1.5 up the blue. The bracket grows one row. Orbit and watch the three dashed pieces stay attached to the axes.
Canvas: view tweens to 3D (cam yaw 35°, pitch 25°, dist 10). The vector resets to [2, 1, 1.5]; `components:'xyz'` draws green, red and blue dashed pieces; labels "2", "1", "1.5"; slow auto-orbit 30° over t.
Math panel: `v = [2; 1; 1.5]` with green, red, blue entries.

### 7. The axis arrows are vectors too · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The axes have their own unit arrows: î = [1; 0; 0], ĵ = [0; 1; 0], k̂ = [0; 0; 1], each of length 1. They are called <b>basis vectors</b>. Every component of <span class="c-v">v</span> says how many copies of one basis vector to walk.
Canvas: the three basis arrows appear from the origin in their colors with hats; their brackets appear beside them; the dashed components of v briefly show as 2 green copies, 1 red copy, 1.5 blue copies laid tip to tail (t∈[0.5,1]).
Math panel: `î = [1;0;0]  ĵ = [0;1;0]  k̂ = [0;0;1]`

### 8. Beyond three: a strip · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Five components — [2; −1; 0.5; 3; 1] — is a point in ℝ⁵. No arrow can be drawn, so we draw the list as a strip of colored cells. Every rule you learn will be stated for the strip, and the strip can be as long as you like. The picture stops at 3. The formula does not.
Canvas: view back to 2D. A 1×5 strip (`g.heat2d`, diverging palette, values printed) slides in; beside it the 3D arrow of the first three components as a small inset labeled "first 3 of 5"; honesty line as a `g.label` under the strip.
Math panel: `v = [2; −1; 0.5; 3; 1] ∈ ℝ⁵`

### 9. Everything in ML is one of these · 5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Three vectors from machine learning. An apartment row from Part 0: [size; rooms; price]. A weight vector: 784 numbers, one per pixel, that a neuron will compare with an image. A word embedding: 300 numbers that encode "cat". All three are arrows we may not be able to draw and lists we always can.
Canvas: three tall brackets drawn side by side as strips: [6.0; 3; 4.2] (3 cells, colored), a 1×784 strip of a weight pattern (gray palette) labeled "w, 784 entries", a 1×300 strip labeled "cat, 300 entries"; each slides in over 1.2 s. Under the first, a tiny 3D inset with its arrow.
Math panel: `x ∈ ℝ³ · w ∈ ℝ⁷⁸⁴ · e_cat ∈ ℝ³⁰⁰`

### 10. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: A vector is one object with three faces: arrow from the origin, point, column of numbers. Its components say how far along each axis, negative meaning the other way. Position does not matter — only length and direction. In more than three dimensions, the list is the picture.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) arrow + point + bracket; (2) ghost copies all labeled the same bracket; (3) a 3D arrow next to a long strip.
Math panel: —

**Playground.** controls: toggle · `view` · 2D/3D · "3D"; preset · `face` · {arrow, point, list, all} · "Face"; toggle · `showComponents` · "Component lines"; matrix · `v` · entries −4..4 step 0.1 · "v (type or drag the tip)"; button · Reset. Presets: "[3; 2]", "[−2; 1.5]", "[2; 1; 1.5] (3D)". Live: the tip handle (`g.handle`, plane 'auto' in 3D), component lines and labels, the bracket.
**Checks.**
1. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Drag the tip to the vector [−2; 3]." · setup: 2D, v = [3, 2], components shown, a faint target ring at (−2, 3) is NOT shown (the learner reads the bracket) · judge: `dist(v, [−2, 3]) < LA.TOL.pos` · hint: "Green component −2 means two steps left; red 3 means three steps up." · solution: tip tweens to (−2, 3).
2. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Make a vector with red component 0 and length 4." · setup: 2D, v = [1, 2] · judge: `|v[1]| < LA.TOL.pos && |‖v‖ − 4| < LA.TOL.pos` (two solutions, ±4 along î) · hint: "Keep the tip on the green axis and read the length." · solution: tip tweens to (4, 0).
3. kind: entry · prompt: "Type the components of the yellow arrow." · bind: `probe` (3-vector) · setup: 3D, v = [1, −2, 2] drawn with component lines but values hidden; the typed vector is drawn orange · answer: [1, −2, 2] · tol: LA.TOL.entry · hint: "Count the green steps, then red (negative goes against ĵ), then blue."
**ML tie-in.** Step 9 previews p1-07 (data as vectors) and p6-02 (embeddings). Bridge: every object a model touches — an input row, a weight vector, an embedding, a gradient — is a vector in exactly this sense. The course's honesty rule starts here: when n > 3 we show the list and compute with it, and we say the arrow is unseen rather than drawing a fake one.
**Recap cards.** (1) "Arrow = point = list" — the three faces side by side. (2) "No position: only length and direction" — ghost copies with identical brackets. (3) "Beyond 3, the list is the picture" — a strip beside a small 3D arrow.

## p1-02-add-scale — Adding and scaling vectors
**Part** 1 · **Tags** core · **Dims** 2D · **Minutes** ~8 · **Prereqs** p1-01-what-is-a-vector
**Goal.** The learner adds vectors tip-to-tail and componentwise, scales them (including through zero to a flip), subtracts them as an arrow between tips, and sees a gradient step as "walk a little along an arrow".
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D · realSize: a gradient step updates 10⁶–10⁹ weights at once with one vector subtraction · transfers: tip-to-tail and componentwise addition are the same rule; in ℝⁿ only the componentwise form is computed, and it is identical.
**Can't show.**
- addition of 784-component vectors as arrows (strips add cell by cell)
**Terms.** vector addition — place the second arrow's tail at the first arrow's tip; the sum runs from the origin to the new tip, and componentwise it adds matching entries; scalar — a plain number used to stretch or shrink a vector; scaling — multiply every component by the same scalar; subtraction — v − w is the arrow from the tip of w to the tip of v; parallelogram rule — v + w is the far corner of the parallelogram with sides v and w; mean (of vectors) — add them and divide by how many; it is a vector.
**State.** `v` ([3, 1]) · `w` ([1, 2]) · `slide` (0..1, how far w has slid to v's tip) · `c` (scalar, default 1) · `showSum` · `showParallelogram` · `showDiff` · `grad` ([0.8, 1.6] gradient arrow for the ML step) · `eta` (0.5) · `target` (gray target vector for checks)

**Steps.**
### 1. Two vectors · 2.5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two vectors from the origin: the yellow <span class="c-v">v</span> = [3; 1] and the purple <span class="c-w">w</span> = [1; 2]. What should it mean to add them? Whatever we choose, it must make sense both for arrows and for lists.
Canvas: 2D range −1..5. v and w grow from the origin (t<0.6) with labels; brackets drawn beside each tip.
Math panel: `v = [3; 1]  w = [1; 2]`

### 2. Tip to tail · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Slide <span class="c-w">w</span>, without turning it, until its tail sits on the tip of <span class="c-v">v</span>. Walk v, then walk w. Where you end up is the sum, <span class="c-v">v</span> + <span class="c-w">w</span>: the arrow from the origin to that final tip, [4; 3].
Canvas: a ghost of w stays at the origin; w translates along v to start at (3, 1) (`slide` 0→1, t∈[0,0.6]); the sum arrow grows from the origin to (4, 3) in a yellow-to-purple gradient stroke (t∈[0.6,1]) with label "v + w".
Math panel: `v + w = [4; 3]`

### 3. Componentwise · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The list face says the same thing. Add the green components: 3 + 1 = 4. Add the red components: 1 + 2 = 3. Matching entries add, nothing crosses rows. For strips of 784 numbers this is the only rule you need.
Canvas: the two brackets slide side by side; the top cells highlight green and their sum "4" appears in the result bracket; then the bottom cells in red give "3". Dashed component lines of the sum show 4 and 3 on canvas.
Math panel: `[3; 1] + [1; 2] = [3+1; 1+2] = [4; 3]` with colored digits.

### 4. Order does not matter · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Walk <span class="c-w">w</span> first and then <span class="c-v">v</span>: you land on the same corner. The two routes form a parallelogram with the sum as its diagonal. v + w = w + v.
Canvas: a second ghost route: v slides to w's tip (1, 2) → (4, 3) (t<0.6); the parallelogram fills faintly (`g.parallelogram(v, w)`, t>0.6) with the diagonal from the origin to (4, 3).
Math panel: `v + w = w + v`

### 5. Lengths do not add · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "adding vectors adds their lengths." ‖<span class="c-v">v</span>‖ ≈ 3.16 and ‖<span class="c-w">w</span>‖ ≈ 2.24, so the sum should be 5.40 long — but ‖v + w‖ = 5 exactly. Lengths add only when the arrows point the same way. Direction matters.
Canvas: a gray bar of length 5.40 lays itself along the sum's direction from the origin and overshoots the tip (t∈[0.2,0.6]); `g.dim` labels "3.16", "2.24", "5.00"; red ✕ on the label "lengths add" at t=0.8.
Math panel: `‖v‖ + ‖w‖ = 5.40 ≠ ‖v + w‖ = 5`

### 6. Scaling: stretch, shrink, flip · 5 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Multiply <span class="c-v">v</span> by a <b>scalar</b> c: every component is multiplied, so the arrow stays on its line. c = 2 doubles it; c = 0.5 halves it; c = 0 collapses it to the origin; c = −1 flips it to point the opposite way. Drag the slider.
Canvas: c tweens 1 → 2 → 0.5 → 0 → −1 (eased, 1.2 s each) unless the learner drags; the yellow arrow c·v stretches along the dashed line through v; the ghost of v stays; label "c·v" and, at c = −1, "−v". Bracket [3c; c] live.
Math panel: `c·v = [3c; c]` with c live, e.g. `−1·[3; 1] = [−3; −1]`.

### 7. Subtraction is an arrow between tips · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: <span class="c-v">v</span> − <span class="c-w">w</span> means v + (−w): flip w and add. The result, [2; −1], is the arrow that goes from the tip of w to the tip of v. "From w to v" — the arrow points at the vector written first. Draw it from the origin when you need its components.
Canvas: −w (dim purple) appears flipped; it slides to v's tip; the result arrow from the origin to (2, −1) draws (t<0.6). Then a copy of the result translates to start at w's tip and ends at v's tip (t∈[0.6,1]), labeled "v − w"; a red ✕ flashes on a gray arrow drawn the wrong way (from v's tip to w's tip).
Math panel: `v − w = [3−1; 1−2] = [2; −1]`

### 8. The midpoint is a vector · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Add <span class="c-v">v</span> and <span class="c-w">w</span> and scale by ½: the result [2; 1.5] lands exactly halfway between the two tips. The <b>mean</b> of vectors is a vector, found by adding and dividing. Averaging a thousand data points works the same way, entry by entry.
Canvas: the sum arrow shrinks by ½ to (2, 1.5); a dashed segment between the tips of v and w with its midpoint marked; the point coincides and pulses.
Math panel: `(v + w)/2 = [2; 1.5]`

### 9. A learning step is "walk along an arrow" · 5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Training a model updates its weight vector <span class="c-w">w</span> by subtracting a small multiple of another vector, the gradient: w ← w − η∇L. That is scaling (by the step size η) and subtraction (walk against the gradient). Every optimizer in machine learning is this picture, repeated, in millions of dimensions.
Canvas: a purple w = [2.5, 1.5]; a gray dashed gradient arrow ∇L = [0.8, 1.6] drawn from w's tip; the step −η∇L (η = 0.5) is laid from the tip backwards along the gradient (t<0.5); the new w draws (t∈[0.5,0.8]); three more faint steps follow along a curved path toward a gold dot labeled "minimum". Label "Part 5: gradient descent".
Math panel: `w ← w − η∇L  (η = 0.5)` with the new w's numbers live.

### 10. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: Adding: tip to tail, or add matching components; the order does not matter. Scaling: multiply every component; negative scalars flip. Subtracting: v − w points from w's tip to v's tip. Lengths do not add unless directions agree. A training step is one scale-and-subtract.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) tip-to-tail with the parallelogram; (2) c·v sliding through the origin; (3) w taking a step against a gradient arrow.
Math panel: —

**Playground.** controls: matrix · `v` · −4..4 · "v (drag tip)"; matrix · `w` · −4..4 · "w (drag tip)"; slider · `c` · −3..3 step 0.05 · "scalar c"; toggle · `showParallelogram` · "Parallelogram"; toggle · `showDiff` · "Show v − w"; button · Reset. Presets: "Same direction (lengths add)", "Perpendicular", "Opposite". Live: sum arrow and bracket, c·v and its bracket, v − w between tips, ‖v‖, ‖w‖, ‖v + w‖ readouts.
**Checks.**
1. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Drag w so that v + w = [1; 4]." · setup: v = [3, 1] fixed, w = [1, 2] draggable, the sum arrow shown live · judge: `dist(v + w, [1, 4]) < LA.TOL.pos` (w ≈ [−2, 3]) · hint: "The sum's green component must drop from 4 to 1: move w 3 to the left." · solution: w tweens to (−2, 3).
2. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Set the slider so that c·v lands on the gray target." · setup: v = [3, 1], gray target arrow at −1.5·v = [−4.5, −1.5], c = 1 · judge: `|c + 1.5| < LA.TOL.scalar` · hint: "The target points the opposite way and is half again as long: c is negative." · solution: c tweens to −1.5.
3. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Drag the gray arrow's tip to equal v − w." · setup: v = [3, 1], w = [1, 2], a gray draggable arrow from the origin starting at [0.5, 0.5] · judge: `dist(gray, v − w) < LA.TOL.pos` (target [2, −1]) · hint: "Subtract matching components: 3 − 1 and 1 − 2." · solution: gray tip tweens to (2, −1).
**ML tie-in.** Step 9 previews p5-03 (gradient descent) and p6-08 (watch a classifier learn). Bridge: the entire training loop of every neural network is `w ← w − η∇L`: a scalar times a vector, subtracted from a vector. The learner has now seen each piece as a picture; Part 5 explains where the gradient arrow comes from and why its length and direction matter.
**Recap cards.** (1) "Add tip to tail, or entry by entry" — parallelogram with diagonal. (2) "Scale: stretch, shrink, flip through 0" — c·v on its dashed line. (3) "w ← w − η∇L is a scaled subtraction" — a step against a gradient arrow.
**Engine needs.** `g.vector` gradient stroke (two-color) is optional; a solid yellow sum arrow with a purple tip label is acceptable.

## p1-03-linear-combinations-span — Linear combinations, span, subspace
**Part** 1 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p1-02-add-scale
**Goal.** The learner sees every vector as a recipe a·v + b·w, watches the set of all recipes paint a plane (or collapse to a line), and can name that flat region a subspace with a dimension.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: a linear model's predictions live in the span of its feature columns — a d-dimensional flat region inside ℝⁿ with n in the thousands · transfers: "span = all recipes" and "a span is flat and passes through the origin" are true in any dimension; only the drawing of the flat region stops at planes in 3D.
**Can't show.**
- a 5-dimensional flat region inside ℝ⁷⁸⁴ (we say "flat, through the origin, 5 directions")
**Terms.** linear combination — a recipe a·v + b·w: scale each vector, then add; span — the set of every vector you can reach by recipes from the given vectors; subspace — a flat region through the origin: a line, a plane, or all of space; it is always a span; collinear — lying on the same line through the origin; dimension (of a subspace) — how many independent directions it takes to sweep it out.
**State.** `v` ([2, 1]) · `w` ([−1, 2]) · `a`, `b` (recipe scalars, default 1, 1) · `trail` (array of reached tips, cleared on setup) · `fill` (0..1 teal span fill) · `wAngle` (for the collapse sweep) · `view` · `cam` · `v3`, `w3`, `u3` (3D vectors [2,0,1], [0,2,1], [0,0,2]) · `uInPlane` (0..1 tween of u3 toward the plane) · `target` (check target)

**Steps.**
### 1. Every vector is a recipe of î and ĵ · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: [2; 3] means "two copies of the green î, then three copies of the red ĵ". Written out, that is 2î + 3ĵ: scale each basis vector, then add. A scale-and-add of vectors is called a <b>linear combination</b>. Every vector in the plane is one.
Canvas: 2D range −4..4. Two green copies of î lay tip to tail from the origin (t<0.4), then three red copies of ĵ (t∈[0.4,0.8]); the yellow arrow to (2, 3) draws over them (t>0.8).
Math panel: `[2; 3] = 2·î + 3·ĵ` with colored terms.

### 2. New ingredients · 3 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Replace the basis arrows by any two: the yellow <span class="c-v">v</span> = [2; 1] and the purple <span class="c-w">w</span> = [−1; 2]. The recipe a·<span class="c-v">v</span> + b·<span class="c-w">w</span> still lands somewhere. Drag the two sliders and watch the tip wander. With a = 1, b = 1 it lands on [1; 3].
Canvas: v and w drawn; a·v (yellow, dashed) then b·w (purple, dashed) tip to tail; the result point gold with label "a·v + b·w"; sliders a, b in the controls are live (interactive); a tweens 1 → 1.5, b 1 → −0.5 automatically if untouched.
Math panel: `a·v + b·w = [2a − b; a + 2b]` with a, b live.

### 3. All recipes paint the plane · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Let a and b sweep through every value. The tips leave a trail of dots, and the dots fill the whole plane: every point is reachable by some recipe. The set of everything reachable is the <b>span</b> of <span class="c-v">v</span> and <span class="c-w">w</span>. Here, the span is the entire plane.
Canvas: a sweeps −2..2 in 9 values and b −2..2 in 9 values (`trail` grows to 81 gold dots in a raster order, t<0.7); the teal fill (`g.polygon` of the visible range, alpha 0.2) fades in behind them (t>0.7) with the label "span = whole plane".
Math panel: `span(v, w) = { a·v + b·w }`

### 4. When two vectors collapse to a line · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Rotate <span class="c-w">w</span> toward the line of <span class="c-v">v</span>. As long as they point in different directions the span is the plane. At the instant they line up — <b>collinear</b> — every recipe stays on one line. The teal plane thins to a teal line; one direction is lost.
Canvas: w rotates from its angle (116.6°) toward v's line (26.6° + 180° = 206.6°), `wAngle` tweened; the trail dots recompute each frame and flatten; the teal fill morphs from the plane into a thin teal line through v at t=1 (the fill's extent perpendicular to v shrinks to 0); label "collinear → span is a line".
Math panel: `w ∥ v  ⇒  span(v, w) = line through v`

### 5. Two vectors do not always reach everything · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "any two vectors reach any point in the plane." With <span class="c-w">w</span> on <span class="c-v">v</span>'s line, try to reach the orange target [0; 1]. Every recipe slides along the teal line and never touches it. Two vectors give two directions only if they are not collinear.
Canvas: w = −2v; orange target point at (0, 1); a and b sweep wildly (the gold tip races along the teal line, t<0.7); the distance to the target is drawn as a dashed gray segment that never closes; red ✕ beside the target at t=0.8 with label "unreachable".
Math panel: `a·v + b·(−2v) = (a − 2b)·v` — always a multiple of v.

### 6. A span is a subspace · 4 s · flags: (none)

## p1-06-dot-product — Dot product: shadow, angle, sum of products, cosine similarity
**Part** 1 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~11 · **Prereqs** p1-05-norms-distance, p0-03-angles-and-circles
**Goal.** The learner sees the dot product three ways — sum of products, shadow length times length, ‖v‖‖w‖cos θ — reads its sign as same-way/perpendicular/opposite, and computes cosine similarity.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: a neuron takes the dot product of a 784-strip with a 784-strip; attention scores are dot products of 64–128 entry queries and keys; embedding search compares 768-entry vectors · transfers: Σvᵢwᵢ is the same sum with more terms; "shadow times length" and the cosine formula remain exactly true in ℝⁿ.
**Can't show.**

**Terms.** dot product (v·w) — one number: multiply matching components and add; equals the shadow of one vector on the other times that other's length; shadow (projection length) — how far along v's line the tip of w falls when dropped perpendicularly; perpendicular (orthogonal) — at a right angle; dot product zero; cosine similarity — the dot product divided by both lengths, the cosine of the angle, from −1 to 1; projection (vector) — the shadow drawn as a vector along v.

Narration: Two vectors: the yellow <span class="c-v">v</span> = [3; 1] and the purple <span class="c-w">w</span> = [1; 2]. The <b>dot product</b> is a single number that answers one question: how much do they point the same way? We will get that number in three different ways and watch them agree.

### 2. Multiply matching components, add · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The list face first. Multiply the green components, 3·1 = 3. Multiply the red ones, 1·2 = 2. Add: 3 + 2 = 5. Written out, <span class="c-v">v</span>·<span class="c-w">w</span> = 3·1 + 1·2 = 5. Nothing crosses rows; it is the simplest possible recipe for a number.

### 3. The shadow · 5 s · flags: (none)

### 4. Either one can cast the shadow · 3 s · flags: (none)

### 5. The sign tells the direction · 6 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Rotate <span class="c-w">w</span> a full turn and watch the number. Same general direction: positive. ### 6. The dot product is not an arrow · 3 s · flags: misconception

### 8. Cosine similarity ignores length · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Divide the lengths out: v·w / (‖v‖‖w‖) = cos θ. This is <b>cosine similarity</b>, a number between −1 and 1 that depends only on directions. Math panel: `cos θ = v·w / (‖v‖‖w‖) = 0.71` with v·w live (5 → 10) and cos fixed.

### 9. The shadow as a vector: projection · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: The shadow itself can be a vector along <span class="c-v">v</span>: the <b>projection</b> of w onto v, proj<sub>v</sub>(w) = (v·w / v·v)·v = 0.5·v = [1.5; 0.5]. If v is a unit vector this is just (v·w)·v. Part 3 builds least squares out of this one arrow.

### 10. In 3D the shadow falls the same way · 5 s · flags: (none)

## p1-08-high-dimensions-measured — High dimensions: what we can't draw, we measure
**Part** 1 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p1-05-norms-distance, p1-06-dot-product, p1-07-data-as-vectors
**Goal.** The learner sees, in charts computed live, that in high dimensions distances concentrate, random directions are nearly perpendicular, volume hides in corners, and images are points whose shadows cluster.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D/3D samples plus charts for d up to 1000 · realSize: embeddings d = 768–4096; images d = 784–150 528 · transfers: everything in this lesson is computed with the strip formulas from p1-05 and p1-06; the charts are measurements, not drawings, and they hold for any d.
**Can't show.**
- a cloud in ℝ¹⁰⁰⁰ (we show its distance and cosine statistics instead)
- a 64-D image cloud (we show a labeled 2D shadow with its variance kept and a reconstruction)
**Terms.** distance concentration — in high dimensions the nearest and farthest points from any point are almost equally far; near-orthogonality — random directions in high dimensions have cosine close to 0; hypercube — the cube with 2ᵈ corners in d dimensions; inscribed sphere — the largest ball that fits inside it; principal component shadow — the 2D projection of a cloud onto its two directions of greatest spread, labeled with the fraction of variance kept.
**State.** `d` (current dimension, from the list [2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000]) · `cache` (per d: nearest/farthest ratio for 200 uniform points, 2000 cosines of random unit pairs, computed at load with `LA.rng(8)`) · `probe` (index 0) · `hist` (bins) · `fracs` (sphere/cube fractions d = 1..20) · `images` (60 8×8 synthetic blobs, 3 templates: horizontal bar, vertical bar, diagonal; noise 0.15, `LA.rng(88)`) · `pca` (2 components, variance fractions) · `recon` (index of the image being reconstructed) · `words` (5 seeded 300-strips for the ML step) · `view` · `cam` · `guess`

**Steps.**
### 1. Nearest and farthest in a square · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Two hundred random points in a square. Pick one, the orange probe, and measure its distance to every other: the nearest is 0.05 away, the farthest 1.3. Nearest divided by farthest is about 0.04 — some points are very close, most are far. That is how distance behaves in a picture.
Canvas: 2D unit square with 200 points (`g.points`); the probe orange; a thin gray segment to the nearest (green ring) and to the farthest (red ring); `g.dim` labels; readout "nearest / farthest = 0.04".
Math panel: `min dist / max dist = 0.04  (d = 2)`

### 2. The same in a cube · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: In a cube with the same two hundred points the ratio climbs a little, to about 0.1: with more room, the nearest neighbor is not quite so near. Orbit the cube. We can still see this one. For the next step we cannot — so we compute.
Canvas: view → 3D; 200 points in the unit cube (`g.points`), the probe and its nearest/farthest segments; auto-orbit 40°; readout for d = 3.
Math panel: `min dist / max dist ≈ 0.10  (d = 3)`

### 3. Distances concentrate · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Repeat the measurement in 5, 10, 100, 1000 dimensions, with no picture, only the formula ‖x − y‖. The ratio nearest/farthest rises toward 1: in a thousand dimensions the closest point is only about 10% closer than the farthest. Everything is roughly equally far from everything. This is <b>distance concentration</b>.
Canvas: 2D; a `g.lineChart` with log-x axis d = 2..1000 and y = ratio; the curve draws point by point from the cache (t<0.8) with a dot at each d; the last value labeled "≈ 0.9"; a dashed line at 1.
Math panel: `ratio(d) = minⱼ‖x₀ − xⱼ‖ / maxⱼ‖x₀ − xⱼ‖` with the current d and value.

### 4. "Nearest neighbor" loses its meaning · 4 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "k-nearest-neighbors works the same in 1000 dimensions as in 2." Here are the probe's distances to all 199 others at d = 1000, sorted: a nearly flat staircase. The nearest and the tenth-nearest differ by a hair; one noisy feature reorders them. Plain distance stops being informative.
Canvas: `g.bars` of the 199 sorted distances at d = 1000 (nearly flat, tall) beside the same for d = 2 (steep); the first ten bars at d = 1000 highlighted; red ✕ at the label "a clear nearest neighbor".
Math panel: `d = 1000: d₁ = 12.6 · d₁₀ = 12.8 · d₁₉₉ = 14.1` (values from the cache).

### 5. Random directions are nearly perpendicular · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Take two random unit vectors and compute their cosine. In 3D the cosines spread across −1 to 1. In 300 dimensions they pile up at 0: almost every pair is nearly <b>perpendicular</b>. The spread shrinks like 1/√d. There is far more room for different directions than in any picture.
Canvas: two `g.hist` panels, 40 bins in [−1, 1]: left d = 3 (broad), right d = 300 (a spike at 0); the right histogram grows from the cache as 2000 pairs are counted (t<0.8); labels "std ≈ 0.50" and "std ≈ 0.06".
Math panel: `cos θ = u·v for random unit u, v · std ≈ 1/√d`

### 6. Room for many concepts · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Near-orthogonality is why embeddings work. In 768 dimensions you can place thousands of concept directions that barely interfere, each almost perpendicular to the rest, while a dot product still picks out the one you ask for. In 3D you could fit only three.
Canvas: 3D: three perpendicular unit arrows (the most you can fit); beside it a 1×16 strip of pairwise |cosines| among 16 random unit vectors in d = 768, all below 0.1, with label "16 of thousands".
Math panel: `|u·v| < 0.1 for all pairs (d = 768, 16 vectors)`

### 7. Volume hides in the corners · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A ball inscribed in a square covers 79% of it. In a cube, 52%. In 10 dimensions the ball fills a quarter of a percent of the cube; by 20, almost nothing. Uniform data in a <b>hypercube</b> lives in its corners, far from the center.
Canvas: left: the unit square with its inscribed circle (filled teal) then the cube with its sphere (3D inset); right: `g.bars` of the fraction for d = 1..20 (log-y), computed by the recursion V_d = (2π/d)·V_{d−2}, V₀ = 1, V₁ = 2, divided by 2ᵈ; bars shrink to invisibility.
Math panel: `fraction(d) = V_d(1)/2ᵈ: 1, 0.79, 0.52, 0.31, 0.16, …, 2.5·10⁻³ (d = 10)`

### 8. Images are points: a shadow that clusters · 6 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Sixty synthetic 8×8 images — three patterns with noise — are sixty points in ℝ⁶⁴. We cannot draw them, but we can compute their two directions of greatest spread and project: the shadow keeps 71% of the variance, and the three patterns fall into three clusters. The picture stops at 3. The formula does not.
Canvas: a 6×10 grid of the tiny images (`g.image`) at the left (t<0.3); each collapses to a point and flies to its 2D PCA coordinates (`LA.num.pca(X, 2)`, t∈[0.3,0.8]), colored by template; label "shadow of ℝ⁶⁴ · 71% of variance kept" (the exact value is printed from the computation); a clicked/hovered point shows its 8×8 image and its 2-component reconstruction side by side (blurry), labeled "reconstruction from 2 numbers"; honesty line.
Math panel: `X: 60×64 · PC₁, PC₂ · variance kept = (λ₁ + λ₂)/Σλ`

### 9. Why embedding search uses cosine · 5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: Five 300-component word vectors. By Euclidean distance the two closest are a long word and a short word that happen to have similar length. By cosine similarity, "cat" and "kitten" win. Distance concentrates and is swayed by length; cosine compares directions, which in high dimensions are plentiful and meaningful. Part 6 builds retrieval on it.
Canvas: five labeled strips (cat, kitten, dog, invoice, tax; seeded with planted structure: cat/kitten cosine 0.9, invoice/tax 0.85, cross 0.1; lengths varied ×0.5–2) and two 5×5 heatmaps (`g.heat2d`): "Euclidean distance" and "cosine similarity"; the top pair in each is outlined gold; they differ. Label "Part 6: embeddings".
Math panel: `cos(cat, kitten) = 0.90 · ‖cat − kitten‖ = 3.2 · ‖cat − dog‖ = 2.9`

### 10. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: High dimensions are measured, not drawn. Distances concentrate, so nearest neighbors blur. Random directions are nearly perpendicular, so there is room for many concepts and cosine stays meaningful. Volume hides in corners. Images are points, and their shadows, honestly labeled, still cluster.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) the ratio curve rising to 1; (2) the spiked cosine histogram; (3) the labeled PCA shadow of the image cloud.
Math panel: —

**Playground.** controls: slider · `d` · index into the d list · "dimension d"; toggle · chart · {distance ratio, cosine histogram, corner fraction} · "Chart"; button · "Resample (new seed)"; slider · `recon` · 0–59 · "Image to reconstruct"; button · Reset. Presets: "d = 3", "d = 300", "Images". Live: the probe's nearest/farthest, the histogram for the current d, the sorted-distance bars, the PCA shadow and the reconstruction.
**Checks.**
1. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Slide d until the cosine histogram's spread (std) falls below 0.1." · setup: histogram chart, d = 3 · judge: `std(cache[d].cosines) < 0.1` (true for d ≥ 200 in the list) · hint: "The spread shrinks like 1/√d; you need d above 100." · solution: d tweens to 200.
2. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Slide d until the nearest/farthest ratio exceeds 0.8." · setup: ratio chart, d = 2 · judge: `cache[d].ratio > 0.8` · hint: "Distances concentrate slowly; go to several hundred dimensions." · solution: d tweens to the first list value whose cached ratio exceeds 0.8.
3. kind: entry · prompt: "Type the fraction of a square covered by its inscribed circle (two decimals)." · bind: `guess` (scalar) · setup: the square and circle drawn; the typed fraction fills the circle's area proportionally in teal · answer: 0.79 · tol: LA.TOL.entry · hint: "π/4."
**ML tie-in.** Step 9 previews p6-02 (embeddings and analogies) and explains the p1-05 k-NN caveat. Bridge: retrieval, recommendation and nearest-neighbor methods all live in hundreds of dimensions where plain distance is nearly uniform; cosine similarity, standardization (Part 5) and dimension reduction (Part 4) are the practitioner's responses to the three measured facts in this lesson.
**Recap cards.** (1) "Distances concentrate: ratio → 1" — the curve. (2) "Random directions are nearly perpendicular: std ≈ 1/√d" — the spike. (3) "Images are points; label the shadow" — PCA scatter with its variance label.
**Engine needs.** `g.lineChart` with a log-x option; `g.hist` with fixed bin edges; `LA.num.pca` on a 60×64 matrix (fast enough at load); load-time computation for the cache must stay under 300 ms (200 points × 10 dimensions × 199 distances plus 2000 cosine pairs per d — precompute only the listed d values).

## p1-09-first-model-neuron — Your first model: a neuron and its boundary
**Part** 1 · **Tags** core · **Dims** both · **Minutes** ~10 · **Prereqs** p1-06-dot-product, p1-07-data-as-vectors
**Goal.** The learner builds a linear classifier by hand: a weight vector perpendicular to its boundary, a bias that slides it, a sign that labels points, a sigmoid that turns the score into a probability — and sees training as moving w.
**Badge.** drawnIn: 2D and 3D · realSize: a digit classifier's last layer is 10 neurons, each with a 784-component w; a boundary in ℝ⁷⁸⁴ is a 783-dimensional hyperplane · transfers: w·x + b, the perpendicularity of w to the boundary, and the sigmoid are identical in any dimension; only the drawing of the boundary stops at a plane.
**Can't show.**
- a hyperplane in ℝ⁷⁸⁴ (we draw the plane in 3D and say the rule is the same)
**Terms.** weight vector (w) — the direction a neuron compares inputs against; score — the number w·x + b, positive on one side of the boundary; decision boundary — the set of points with score 0, a line perpendicular to w; bias (b) — a number added to the score that slides the boundary without turning it; sigmoid — a smooth S-shaped function squashing any score into a probability between 0 and 1; hyperplane — the boundary in n dimensions: a line in 2D, a plane in 3D, flat in general; training — adjusting w and b to lower the number of mistakes.
**State.** `data` (`twoBlobs40`) · `w` ([1, −1]) · `b` (0) · `showScores` (bars) · `showBoundary` · `showSigmoid` (3D surface) · `data3` (3D version: means [−1.5, 1, 0.5] and [1.5, −1, −0.5], sd 0.6) · `w3` ([1, −1, −0.5]) · `trainPath` (precomputed list of (w, b) over 8 steps) · `trainStep` (0..8) · `probe` (gold point for check 3) · `view` · `cam`

**Steps.**
### 1. Two kinds of points · 3 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Forty points, two kinds: blue and rose, from the dataset of the last lesson's kind. We want a rule that, given a new point, says which kind it is. The rule will be built from one vector and one number — your first model.
Canvas: 2D range −4..4; the two clouds fade in (`g.points`, data/dataB colors); a gray question-mark point at (0.3, 0.2).
Math panel: `x ∈ ℝ², label ∈ {blue, rose}`

### 2. A weight vector scores every point · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Pick a purple <b>weight vector</b> <span class="c-w">w</span> = [1; −1]. Score every point by the dot product w·x: its shadow on w, times ‖w‖. Points on w's side of the origin score positive; points behind score negative. Bars show the scores.
Canvas: w drawn from the origin; six sample points drop dashed residuals onto w's line with magenta shadows (t<0.5); `g.bars` for all 40 scores appear at the right, blue bars mostly negative, rose mostly positive (t>0.5).
Math panel: `score(x) = w·x` with three example values.

### 3. The boundary is perpendicular to w · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Where is the score exactly 0? On the line through the origin perpendicular to <span class="c-w">w</span> — every point there has no shadow. That line is the <b>decision boundary</b>. Color each point by the sign of its score: the rule is "positive means rose". It already gets 37 of 40 right.
Canvas: the gold boundary line x − y = 0 draws perpendicular to w with a right-angle marker (t<0.4); the points recolor by sign (filled = correct, ringed = wrong) (t∈[0.4,0.8]); accuracy label "37/40".
Math panel: `w·x = 0 ⇔ boundary · sign(w·x) = label`

### 4. w does not lie along the boundary · 3 s · flags: misconception
Narration: Belief: "the weight vector points along the boundary line." Try it: a gray arrow laid along the line scores the points on the line at full strength, not zero. The weight vector is the <i>normal</i> — it points across the boundary, at the side it calls positive.
Canvas: a gray arrow along the boundary direction [1, 1] appears with label "w?"; a point on the boundary drops its shadow onto this gray arrow and gets a nonzero bar; red ✕ at t=0.7; the purple w pulses with the right-angle marker.
Math panel: `w ⟂ boundary`

### 5. The bias slides the boundary · 4 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Add a number b to every score: w·x + b. The boundary slides along <span class="c-w">w</span> by −b/‖w‖ without turning. With b = −1 it moves toward the rose side and fixes two mistakes: 39 of 40. The <b>bias</b> lets the boundary leave the origin.
Canvas: b tweens 0 → −1; the gold line translates along w's direction (ghost of the old line stays dashed); `g.dim` "−b/‖w‖ = 0.71"; the recolored points update; accuracy "39/40".
Math panel: `score(x) = w·x + b · b = −1`

### 6. Separate them yourself · 6 s · flags: interactive
Narration: Drag the tip of <span class="c-w">w</span> to turn the boundary and use the slider to slide it. Watch the accuracy counter. A direction that points from the blue cloud toward the rose cloud, with a small negative b, separates all forty. There are many correct answers.
Canvas: `g.handle` on w's tip; b slider live; boundary, point colors and the accuracy label update every frame; a demo drag (if untouched) rotates w from [1, −1] to [1.3, −0.9] and sets b = −0.4.
Math panel: `w = [w₁; w₂], b` live with `accuracy`.

### 7. From score to probability: the sigmoid · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: A score of 7 and a score of 0.2 are both "rose", but not equally sure. The <b>sigmoid</b> σ(s) = 1/(1 + e<sup>−s</sup>) squashes any score into a probability between 0 and 1. Over the plane it is a smooth ramp, steep along <span class="c-w">w</span> and flat along the boundary, crossing 0.5 exactly on the gold line.
Canvas: view → 3D (cam yaw 30°, pitch 35°); `g.surface` z = σ(w·x + b) over the range (res 30, translucent); the data points lifted to their probability heights; the gold boundary drawn as the 0.5 contour on the surface; a small 2D inset of the S-curve.
Math panel: `p(rose) = σ(w·x + b) = 1/(1 + e^{−(w·x + b)})`

### 8. In 3D the boundary is a plane; in general, a hyperplane · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: With three features the same rule gives a plane: all points with w·x + b = 0, perpendicular to <span class="c-w">w</span>. With 784 features it is a <b>hyperplane</b> we cannot draw, but w is still the normal and the score is still one dot product. The picture stops at 3. The formula does not.
Canvas: 3D data (`data3`); w3 from the origin; the gold plane (`g.planes`, normal w3, through −b·w3/‖w3‖² with b = −0.5) with the points colored by side; auto-orbit 50°; honesty line.
Math panel: `w·x + b = 0 in ℝ³: a plane · in ℝⁿ: a hyperplane`

### 9. Training is moving w · 5 s · flags: (none)
Narration: Start with a bad guess and let the data push. Each step changes <span class="c-w">w</span> and b a little in the direction that lowers the mistakes — the scaled subtraction from p1-02. Eight steps swing the boundary from wrong to right. Part 5 explains where the push comes from.
Canvas: 2D; `trainPath` precomputed in setup (logistic loss, full batch, learning rate 0.5, from w₀ = [0.2, 0.8], b₀ = 0, 8 steps); w and the boundary tween through the 8 states (0.5 s each) leaving faint ghosts; accuracy counter climbs; the w-tip path drawn as a dotted gold trail.
Math panel: `w ← w − η∇L` with step counter and accuracy.

### 10. Ten neurons read a digit · 5 s · flags: mlTie
Narration: A digit classifier's last layer is ten of these neurons, each with a 784-component w, scoring the same flattened image. The ten scores become ten bars; the tallest wins. Logistic regression, the perceptron and the output layer of every network are this picture. Part 6 turns the bars into probabilities that sum to 1.
Canvas: the synthetic "7" strip (1×784) at the left; ten weight strips stacked (seeded patterns, `LA.rng(99)`, with the 7-template planted in row 7); each dot product fills a bar in a `g.bars` panel; bar 7 tallest, gold; label "Part 6: softmax classifier".
Math panel: `scoreₖ = wₖ·x + bₖ, k = 0..9`

### 11. Recap · 3 s · flags: recap
Narration: A neuron is a weight vector, a dot product and a bias: score = w·x + b. The boundary is the zero set, perpendicular to w; b slides it. The sign labels points; the sigmoid turns the score into a probability. Training moves w. In ℝⁿ the boundary is a hyperplane and the formula is unchanged.
Canvas: three mini-cards: (1) w with its perpendicular gold line and colored points; (2) the sigmoid ramp surface; (3) a strip against ten weight strips with bars.
Math panel: —

**Playground.** controls: matrix · `w` · "w (drag tip)"; slider · `b` · −4..4 step 0.05 · "bias b"; toggle · `showScores` · "Score bars"; toggle · `showSigmoid` · "Sigmoid surface (3D)"; button · "Train 1 step" (advances `trainStep`); button · Reset. Presets: "Untrained", "Trained", "Vertical boundary". Live: boundary, point colors, accuracy, score bars, the sigmoid surface, −b/‖w‖ dimension.
**Checks.**
1. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Turn and slide the boundary until all 40 points are on the correct side." · setup: w = [0.2, 0.8], b = 0 (accuracy 21/40) · judge: every point has sign(w·x + b) matching its class (accuracy 40/40) · hint: "Point w from the blue cloud toward the rose cloud, then nudge b." · solution: w, b tween to the trained values from `trainPath[8]`.
2. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Make the boundary vertical." · setup: w = [1, −1], b = 0 · judge: `angle(w, ±î) < LA.TOL.angleDeg` · hint: "A vertical line has a horizontal normal: w along the green axis." · solution: w tweens to [1.4, 0].
3. kind: manipulate · prompt: "Drag the gold probe point onto the decision boundary." · setup: w = [1, −1], b = −1, probe at (2, 2) · judge: `|w·probe + b| / ‖w‖ < LA.TOL.pos` · hint: "Move it until its score bar reads 0." · solution: probe tweens to its perpendicular foot on the line.
**ML tie-in.** Step 10 previews p6-04 (softmax classifier) and p6-08 (watch a classifier learn); step 9 previews p5-03 (gradient descent). Bridge: this neuron is the unit every later architecture is built from — a layer is many neurons (p2-12), attention scores are these dot products (p6-03), and training is the step of p1-02 applied to w. The learner has now seen the full loop data → score → boundary → adjust, in pictures only.
**Recap cards.** (1) "score = w·x + b; boundary ⟂ w; b slides it" — arrow, line, points. (2) "sigmoid: score → probability" — the S ramp. (3) "Ten neurons = ten dot products = a classifier" — strips and bars.
**Engine needs.** `g.surface` with a translucent fill and the 0.5 contour drawn on it (`g.contours` on the same fn at level 0.5, projected onto the surface — or simply draw the boundary line lifted to z = 0.5).
